Japanese vowels, first pass
===========================

Five sustained Japanese vowels synthesised from measured formant targets, to
hear whether the vowel qualities are right before any consonant work starts.

16 kHz, mono.  Nothing here is speech yet: each file is one parameter frame
held, so the amplitude is flat and the pitch is a steady 100 Hz.  It will sound
buzzy and mechanical -- that is what a held frame is.  What to listen for is
whether each one is recognisably the Japanese vowel it claims to be, and in
particular whether /u/ sounds Japanese rather than European.

  01-a.wav  02-i.wav  03-u.wav  04-e.wav  05-o.wav
        the five vowels on their own, ~0.8 s each

  06-aiueo.wav
        the five in the あいうえお order, with gaps

  11-aiueo-mora-paced.wav
        the same five at ~140 ms each, which is roughly the 7 morae/s the
        prosody papers use as a normal speaking rate.  This is what Japanese
        timing feels like, without consonants.

  07-u-fronted.wav / 08-u-backed.wav / 09-u-allophones.wav
        /u/ has two allophones.  Fronted (F2 1323 Hz) is what it is after
        s, sh, ts, z, y, ky; backed (F2 1081 Hz) after k, b, m.  09 alternates
        them -- fronted, backed, fronted, backed.  A single /u/ target would be
        audibly wrong in one context or the other.

  10-u-japanese-vs-borrowed.wav
        Japanese /u/ (F2 1293 Hz) against the engine's own English /uw/ F2 of
        1020 Hz, everything else held identical so only F2 differs.  Alternates
        Japanese, borrowed, Japanese, borrowed.  This is the sound of the
        decision not to reuse an existing inventory.


Where the numbers come from
---------------------------

Targets are the pooled medians of Mokhtari & Tanaka (2000), "A corpus of
Japanese vowel formant patterns", ETL Bulletin -- five adult male native
speakers, 2750 patterns, F1-F4 and B1-B4 in Hz.

They reach the engine as parameter tracks 9-12 (F1-F4) and 13-15 (B1-B3),
through the scalings measured out of the engine: F1 is value x4, F2 is
value x8 + 500, F3 and F4 are value x16, and the bandwidths are value x2.

Synthesised, then measured back by LPC.  Target -> what the byte can ask for
-> what came out:

  vowel      F1                 F2                 F3                 F4
    a    737 > 736 > 703   1225 >1228 >1239   2275 >2272 >2278   3304 >3312 >3251
    i    298 > 300 > 298   2067 >2068 >2087   2951 >2944 >2930   3455 >3456 >3345
    u    356 > 356 > 372   1293 >1292 >1305   2224 >2224 >2216   3282 >3280 >3283
    e    481 > 480 > 490   1873 >1876 >1885   2406 >2400 >2390   3381 >3376 >3301
    o    456 > 456 > 474    856 > 860 > 861   2343 >2336 >2324   3246 >3248 >3272

The middle column is quantisation and is small everywhere -- F1 sits on a 4 Hz
grid and F2 on an 8 Hz grid.  The right column is the LPC reading of the actual
audio, and it includes the estimator's own error: /a/'s F1 reads 34 Hz low,
which is the worst of the twenty and is most likely the measurement rather than
the synthesis, since /a/ carries a 170 Hz B1 and a wide resonance is the hardest
kind to place.

Generated by the engine at build/bin/tvtts64.dll through the parameter escapes
documented in docs/VOICES.md:

    ESC[<track>;<value>l  ...  ESC[<frames>g

This whole directory is build output and is not committed.

Consonants, and a first word
----------------------------

  12-konnichiwa.wav    こんにちは  (ko-N-ni-chi-wa), ~0.8 s
  13-wa.wav            /wa/ on its own
  14-ya.wav            /ya/ on its own

These are a different kind of thing from the vowels above, and the difference
matters.  The five vowel targets are *measured*, from a published corpus of
Japanese.  The consonant postures here are **authored from phonetic principle**
-- nasal murmur at a low first formant, a velar locus before a back vowel, a
labiovelar glide, a palatoalveolar noise spectrum -- and nothing in jp_res/
supports them.  They are a plausible first guess, not data.

What the word is built from, and what came back out of the audio:

  segment          planned                        measured
  k closure        silence                        silent
  k release        F2 falling from a velar locus  1098 -> 915 Hz
  o                456 / 856                      455 / 838
  N (moraic)       nasal murmur, F1 ~250, quiet   262 / 1705, 4 of 22 level
  n -> i           glide to /i/                   F2 1807 -> 1991
  i                298 / 2067                     309 / 2052
  ch closure       silence                        silent
  ch release       sibilant, energy above 3.5 kHz 0.83 of the spectrum
  i                298 / 2067                     307 / 2060
  i -> w           F2 down to a labiovelar         1488 -> 585
  w -> a           F1 and F2 both rising          535 -> 706, 893 -> 1232
  a                737 / 1225                     697 / 1230

So the word has two real stop closures, a nasal with the right first-formant
drop, a sibilant release, and three vowels that land within about 40 Hz of
target with genuine transitions between them.  Whether it sounds like
こんにちは is the part no measurement settles.


How it had to be rendered, which is a finding
---------------------------------------------

The engine follows only about **ten frames** of queued parameter changes in one
utterance.  Past that it stops honouring them and holds whatever it last had --
measured in every configuration tried: one escape per frame or seven, short
holds or long.  Holds of more than one frame are worse: they freeze every
parameter change after the first.

So these files are rendered **one segment per utterance** and concatenated, with
the stop closures inserted as explicit silence rather than asked of the engine
(trimming each utterance to its sounding part would otherwise delete them, and
a stop without its closure is not a stop).  Joins are crossfaded over 2 ms.

That is a workaround, and it says where the real boundary is: the text-escape
path is good for experiments up to about ten frames, and a language cannot be
driven through it.  Japanese needs a frame-level entry point in C that writes
the 22 tracks and calls Synth_Frame directly, which is the architecture that was
planned anyway -- the escapes were only ever the cheap way to prove the seam.


The affricate, and why a centre of gravity is not enough
--------------------------------------------------------

  15-konnichiwa-ch-variants.wav   dark / SHIPPED / sharp, in that order

The first build matched Tanaka's measured centre of gravity for [tC] almost
exactly -- 4467 Hz against ~4400 -- and still sounded too sharp.  A centroid is
one number, and two noises that share one can be quite different: that version
put **26-29% of its energy above 6 kHz** and had its spectral peak down at 2450,
nowhere near where the energy sat.

The parallel branch runs on the same formant frequencies as the cascade, so the
fix is to shape the noise by putting it on F3/F4 -- bands 5 and 6 -- instead of
on track 8's raw 4-8 kHz hiss:

  version            CoG    peak    3-4 kHz   above 6 kHz
  sharp (first)      4281    2445      0.31      0.26
  SHIPPED            4155    3578      0.67      0.16
  dark               3682    3867      0.83      0.04

Three were built and listened to and the middle was chosen by ear.  It is also
the one nearest Tanaka's published figure.  So the ear and the measurement do
agree here -- once the measurement is not asked to carry the whole description
on its own.  What the first version got wrong was never the centroid; it was
the quarter of the energy sitting in the top octave, which a centroid averages
away.


The F0 contour: the Fujisaki model
-----------------------------------

  fujisaki.py       the model and the published symbol values
  make_konnichiwa.py  uses it for track 17

    ln F0(t) = ln Fb + SUM Api*Gp(t-T0i) + SUM Aaj*[Ga(t-T1j) - Ga(t-T2j)]

Phrase and accent are both critically-damped second-order mechanisms, driven by
an impulse and a step respectively (Hirose et al., SSW2 1994).  The two rate
constants are NOT in the papers here -- they are referenced to Fujisaki & Hirose
1984, which we do not have -- so alpha was pinned from the figure instead of
taken on trust: in SSW2 Fig.1(a) the phrase component turns up at 0.78 s and
peaks at 1.25 s, and Gp peaks at exactly 1/alpha, so that 0.47 s delay gives
alpha = 2.1.  2.0 is used, which is also the commonly published value.
beta = 20 /s and theta = 0.9 are the published figures.

Checked: Gp peaks at 0.500 s against 1/alpha = 0.500, and Ga reaches theta in
195 ms.  Both exact.  Reproducing Fig.1(a)'s three phrase commands gives a
72-157 Hz contour of the right shape against the figure's ~62-185; the command
magnitudes there were read off a scan by eye, so that is a sanity check and not
a fit.

For konnichiwa the accent comes from the dictionary rather than a guess:
naist-jdic has it as **0/5** -- heiban, five morae, no nucleus -- so by Kawai
section 7.2 the accent command rises after mora 1 and falls after the final
mora, with no downstep between.  One phrase command P1 = 0.35 placed at -0.25 s
so its peak lands near mora 2, where Japanese puts the rise.  Fb = 72 Hz, inside
the 58-73 Hz that Hirose et al. measured for male readers.

Measured back out of the audio, model against rendered:

   t      0.04   0.28   0.38   0.50   0.76
   model    90    122    144    143    136
   audio    92    136    144    144    134


A front end: text in, audio out
--------------------------------

    python build/Japanese_test/jp_speak.py "konnichiwa" out.wav [accent]

Romaji or kana, and `accent` is the Tokyo accent type -- 0 for heiban, n for a
nucleus on mora n.  Three modules:

  jp_mora.py    kana or romaji -> morae.  Counts in morae, not syllables, so
                kya is one and the three special morae (N, Q, long vowel) each
                take one of their own.  nippon -> ni Q po N, toukyou -> to : kyo :
  jp_voice.py   the phoneme inventory.  Every entry is marked with where it
                came from: [M] the vowel corpus, [T] Tanaka's measurements,
                [L] the general literature, [E] estimated from place with no
                source found.
  jp_speak.py   morae -> frames -> tvtts_speak_frames, with mora timing at the
                papers' ~7 morae/s, 30 ms formant transitions, high-vowel
                devoicing between voiceless consonants, and the Fujisaki
                contour placed by Kawai's section 7.2 timing rules.

  20..28  konnichiwa, arigatou, sakura, neko, nihon, toukyou, sumimasen,
          sensei, nippon -- each with its real accent type from naist-jdic
  29      hashi + ga, three ways

The accents are looked up, not guessed: naist-jdic carries accent/mora_count per
entry, so arigatou is 2/5, neko is 1/2, sumimasen is 4/5, toukyou is 0/4.

**29 is the one to listen to.**  The classic Tokyo triple needs the particle to
show itself -- in isolation 2/2 and 0/2 are genuinely identical, and only the
following *ga* separates them:

                       mora1   mora2   ga
   hashi-ga 1  chopsticks  124    109    92     high, then down
   hashi-ga 2  bridge       92    125   108     up, then down on the particle
   hashi-ga 0  edge         92    125   145     up, and stays up

That is the accent system doing what it is supposed to do, end to end: kana in,
dictionary accent, Fujisaki commands placed by rule, 22 tracks a frame, audio.


The sibilants, and a mistake worth keeping
-------------------------------------------

  30-s-vs-sh.wav        sa/sha, su/shu, so/sho, then the full sa- and sha- rows
  31-sibilant-words.wav sushi, shashin, sensei, tsuki, chikara, sayounara

The first version put the sibilant noise on the parallel bands, which sit on the
**vowel's** F3 and F4.  That is wrong twice over.  A fricative resonates in its
own front cavity, not in the vowel's formants -- so the noise peak followed the
vowel around instead of staying where the constriction is -- and /s/'s cavity
sits at 5-8 kHz, which is above this engine's F4 ceiling of 4080 Hz and
therefore cannot be a formant at all.

The result was /s/ peaking at 3273 Hz, which is [C] territory, so /s/ and /sh/
were barely distinguishable; and because the peak was too low the sound needed
raw track-8 hiss to brighten it, which left **32% of the energy above 6 kHz**.
Low peak plus top-octave hiss is exactly what "too sharp" sounds like.

Now each sibilant carries its own upper formants (SIB_POST in jp_voice.py):
/s/ is pushed as high as the tracks reach and carried by band 6 alone, [C] sits
at 3000/3700 on bands 5 and 6.

                  peak    3-4k   4-5k   above 6k
   /s/   sa       4672    0.03   0.67     0.01
   /sh/  shi      3750    0.77   0.17     0.02
   [tC]  chi      3766    0.70   0.22     0.02
   [ts]  tsu      4375    0.04   0.90     0.01

922 Hz between /s/ and [C], and the top octave is down from 32% to 1%.  Compare
against the published description: /s/ peaks at 5-6 kHz and [C] at 3-4 kHz while
their centres of gravity are nearly equal, 4500 against 4400 -- which is the
third time in this work that a centre of gravity has agreed while the sound did
not.  The peak is the part that carries the identity.


Stop bursts, from a pole-zero model
------------------------------------

  32-stops-pa-ta-ka.wav   pa ta ka / pi ti ki / pu tsu ku / po to ko
  33-stop-words.wav       kasa, takai, pikapika, tokei, kutsu, poketto

Morikawa (1997), J. Acoust. Soc. Jpn. (E) 18(4) -- 270 Japanese CV syllables
analysed with a pole-zero (ARMA) model.  He describes the burst as a real-axis
pole Fp0, a first pole Fp1 and its bandwidth Bp1, which is already a formant
specification, so it ports straight over.  His synthesiser is "equivalent to the
terminal analog type digital filter with poles and zeros" -- the same family as
this one.  Three templates, from his Fig. 1:

  /p/  diffuse-falling, a constant -8 dB/octave, INDEPENDENT of the vowel,
       carried by a real pole under 300 Hz
  /t/  nearly flat across 0-5 kHz
  /k/  a sharp strong peak -- the most resonant of the three -- which moves
       with the following vowel

Morikawa gives the shape and Tanaka gives the centre frequency per vowel, so the
two together fix both.  Measured back out of the audio, 16 ms at the burst:

           peak    want    slope dB/oct
   /pa/     688     600      -11.2
   /po/     688     600      -11.2     (vowel-independent, as he reports)
   /pi/    1188    1200       -2.6     (/pi/ is really /pji/, so it differs)
   /ta/    1188    flat       +1.8     spread 0.13 / 0.39 / 0.17 / 0.32
   /ka/    1188    1300      -10.7
   /ki/    3062    3100       +4.3
   /ku/    1125    1100      -13.4
   /ko/     688     800      -16.9

Two engine limits shaped this.  **F2 stops at 2540 Hz**, so a velar burst before
/i/ at 3100 cannot sit on it and has to go on F3.  And **there is no B4 track**
-- bandwidths are B1 to B3 only -- so F4 cannot be damped and always rings
wherever it is put.  Leaving it at the 4080 ceiling put /ke/'s burst at 4062 Hz
when it belongs at 2100; setting it just above the peak instead makes it
reinforce that peak rather than form a second lump.

Caveat on the source: Morikawa's material was low-passed at 4.8 kHz and sampled
at 10 kHz, so nothing above 5 kHz is in it.  A real /t/ burst carries energy
higher than that, and the flatness reported is flatness within 0-5 kHz.


The vowel before a geminate
----------------------------

  40-sokuon-offglide.wav   ata/atta, ita/itta, kata/katta, toru/tooru,
                           kite/kitte

Yanagisawa & Arai (2015) synthesised /ata/ ~ /atta/ in **XKL**, a Klatt
synthesiser, and varied two things in the preceding vowel's off-glide while
sweeping the closure from 100 to 380 ms.  Their findings:

  * the formant transition IS a cue.  The closure at which listeners called it
    a geminate half the time moved from **184 ms with the transition to 240 ms
    without** -- the transition alone is worth **56 ms of closure**.  Without
    it, even a 380 ms closure was only heard as a geminate 80% of the time.
  * the *rate* of intensity damping is NOT much of a cue to the geminate.

Their off-glide was F1 750 -> 500 and F2 1250 -> 1400.

Two things came out of checking against that.  First, this synthesiser's
geminate branch had **no off-glide at all** -- the vowel ran flat into the
closure, which is precisely their no-transition condition, the one that costs
56 ms.  It has one now, aimed at the consonant being doubled.

Second, the /t/ locus was wrong in kind.  Tanaka's numbers for /k/ and /p/ are
F2 measured *at release*, which is a value the formants actually reach; the
1800 Hz being used for /t/ came from locus theory, which is a virtual target
the formants only travel partway toward.  Mixing the two moved /a/'s F2 by
575 Hz where the measured off-glide moves it by 150.  The alveolars are now
attained values anchored on their 1400.

Measured after, through the /a/ of atta:

   F1  694 -> 483        (they synthesised 750 -> 500)
   F2 1221 -> 1454       (they synthesised 1250 -> 1400)

Note the damping result does not undo the taper added from Kashino (1992): his
finding is that the envelope cues the consonant's **manner and voicing**, which
is a different contrast from singleton-versus-geminate.  The two coexist, and
the taper is not tuned for the geminate.


The flap /r/
------------

  41-r-subphonemes.wav     ara, as Arai's (b) flap, (c) approximant, (d) lateral flap
  42-r-dip-depth.wav       ara, the flap at AVd -10, -25, -40 dB
  43-r-before-after.wav    arigatou, sakura, hiragana -- approximant then flap
  44-r-vowels.wav          ara, iri, uru, ere, oro
  45-r-words.wav           arigatou, sakura, chikara, hiragana, ringo, midori,
                           kuruma, sarasara

/r/ is the second most frequent consonant ONSET in naist-jdic, counting one
contribution per entry: 11.41% of 1,776,191 onsets over all 486,646 pronounced
entries, behind /k/ 18.20% and ahead of /m/ 9.70%, /s/ 7.51% and /t/ 6.97%.
That rank is weighting-dependent, and no weighting of a dictionary measures
running speech: count each distinct pronunciation string once instead and /r/
is THIRD at 9.88%, behind /k/ 17.00% and /m/ 9.97%.  It had no source behind it
at all.

Takayuki Arai, "Perceptual Cues of Japanese /r/ Sounds: Formant Transitions vs.
Intensity Dip", LabPhon 14, NINJAL Tokyo, is as close to a specification as a
paper gets: 195 /ara/ stimuli from a formant synthesiser, twenty listeners
rating /r/-likeness 0-100%, four parameters.

  AVd  depth of the intensity dip                 0 to -40 dB
  AVg  duration of the intensity dip              20-100 ms
  Fg   total duration of the formant transitions  20-100 ms, Fg >= AVg
  F2p  peak of F2                                 1200-1600 Hz

His carrier vowel was F1 750 / F2 1200 and F1 falls to 500 through the
constriction.  Mokhtari's /a/ here is 737 / 1225, so his numbers transfer
unscaled -- the one piece of luck in this.

The regions scoring 70% or better are not one region.  They are three, and he
calls them "sub-phonemes" of real /r/, each heard as a good Japanese /r/:

  (b) alveolar flap          AVd -10..-40  AVg 20-40  Fg 60-100  F2p 1400-1600
  (c) lateral approximant    no dip                   Fg 80-100  F2p 1400
  (d) lateral flap           no dip                   Fg 40-60   F2p 1600

The useful result is the negative one.  At F2p = 1200, that is with no F2
movement at all, scores were LOW: the rise toward 1400-1600 is what carries the
tongue's move to the alveolar ridge.  And the dip is what separates a flap from
an approximant -- "the intensity dip induces a sensation of the flap sound".

Measured on the /ara/ this code produced before reading him:

   F2  1228 -> 1404 -> 1228       Fg 80 ms       AV flat at 60, no dip

which is (c) almost exactly.  That is worth saying plainly: the /r/ was not
broken, it was the lateral approximant -- a legitimate allophone that most of
his listeners accepted -- rather than the flap, which is his typical phone in
the intervocalic position and the one Japanese is usually described as having.
F1 was the one plain error, going to 400 where he puts the constriction at 500.

Now, with the defaults (mode 'flap', AVd 25, the middle of his range):

   F2  1228 -> 1500 -> 1228       Fg 80 ms       AV 60 -> 35 -> 60 over 30 ms

and measured back off the rendered audio rather than off the parameter tracks:

   F2 rises 1231 -> 1588 by LPC   intensity dips 22.7 dB

Only /a/ was tested, so the other four vowels are extrapolated the standard
way, a locus equation through an alveolar locus of 1800 Hz with k fitted to the
single point he measured:

   a 1500   i 1939   u 1535   e 1838   o 1307      (was 1400/1850/1450/1700/1250)

jp_voice.set_flap(mode, avd=) switches sub-phoneme, which is what 41 and 42 are
rendered with.

Still not done from this paper: he says phrase-initial /r/ "is often produced as
a plosive-like sound".  That is a positional allophone, and every /r/ here is
the intervocalic one wherever it falls.


The palatalised flap /ry/
-------------------------

  46-ry-words.wav          ryokou, ryouri, ryokan, ryuukou, ryouhou

Checking the flap turned up something worse than a wrong value.  /ry/ had no
MANNER entry and no LOCUS entry, which made it the one consonant the front end
could emit and the synthesiser could not say: rya/ryu/ryo fell through to
('none',1,0,0) and came out as a bare glide from a default 1500 Hz posture with
no flap in them at all.  Over all 486,646 pronounced naist-jdic entries it is
1.02% of consonant onsets -- NOT level with /z/ and /j/, as this file used to
say: they are 2.00% and 1.98%, twice its share.  It is the ONLY gap in the
inventory, and the same scan now comes back clean.

Its locus is anchored on Tanaka's MEASURED palatalised stops, /kj/ 2200-2300 and
/pj/ 2080-2250, rather than on his alveolo-palatal fricatives at 2420-2580:
those hold a front-cavity constriction that one brief contact never attains.
With the locus equation at 2150 and the same k:

   a 1667   i 2107   u 1718   e 2005   o 1475

/u/ starts from the fronted 1323 rather than the citation 1293, because /ry/ is
in _FRONTING and the flap should not be made to travel from a value the vowel
does not begin at.


Sentences: phrasing, downstep, final lowering
----------------------------------------------

  47-sentences.wav         four sentences of two to four accent phrases
  48-downstep.wav          Kubozono's (23): umai nomimono / amai nomimono,
                           then umai nomimono again with downstep disabled
  49-final-lowering.wav    konnichiwa and a sentence, each without P0 then with

Everything above this point is one word.  That was not a limitation of the
voice but of pitch(): it emitted exactly one Fujisaki phrase command and one
accent command, so an utterance was a single accent phrase by construction.

Input now has two levels of boundary above the mora.  A space is an ACCENT
PHRASE boundary: no pause, the articulation runs straight through, but the
pitch gets a fresh accent command and downstep chains across it.  A bar is a
MAJOR PHRASE boundary: a pause, a new phrase command, and downstep resets.  A
double bar is the stronger clause-level one.  Kawai's rules (1)-(6) decide
where these belong from clause and ICRLB boundaries, which needs a parser we do
not have, so the symbols are exposed rather than derived.

**Downstep**, from Kubozono (1987) ch. 5.  Three properties, each checked:

  * it is ACCENT-induced.  A word is lowered when it follows an ACCENTED word,
    not merely another word.  Measured here: with three accented phrases the
    third peaks at 0.781 of the first; put a heiban phrase in the middle and it
    is 0.890, because the heiban phrase does not trigger a step.
  * it CHAINS within the major phrase and resets at a major phrase boundary.
  * it is a shift of RANGE, not an ordering, so a downstepped phrase can still
    be phonetically higher than the one that lowered it.

His Table 22 puts the inter-peak ratio at 141.1/157.7 = 0.895 and finds it near
constant across tonal structures -- which is the signature of a fixed
multiplier rather than one tracking the accentual fall.  DOWNSTEP = 0.80 is
fitted to reproduce that on his own (26a), and lands at 0.894.  It is fitted on
TOP of the phrase component's own decay, which alone gives 0.967, so the two
are not double-counted.

His (23) is a minimal pair for the trigger and it is sample 48:

   uma'i nomimono   accented first word    nomimono peaks 119.9 Hz
   amai  nomimono   unaccented first word  nomimono peaks 144.4 Hz

**Final lowering.** Kawai rule (1): at a sentence boundary place P1 and SP1, but
at the END of the text place only P0 -- which is NEGATIVE, -0.50.  Nothing was
emitting it, so every utterance ended as high as its last accent left it.  With
it, the fall over the last 0.3 s goes from 7.2 to 42.2 Hz on konnichiwa and
from 4.3 to 35.5 Hz on watashiwa gakuseidesu.

**The L1 threshold, which turned out not to be a detail.** Giving every major
phrase its own command made the second phrase peak ABOVE the first.  The cause
is in the model: at ALPHA = 2.0 the phrase response does not peak until 0.5 s
after its impulse, so a command fired half a second later lands on a first that
has not begun to decay.  Measured against phrase length:

   3 morae  ratio 1.063      8 morae  ratio 0.891
   4 morae  ratio 0.992     11 morae  ratio 0.842

Kawai's rules (2)-(5) share a clause that is exactly this condition -- if the
distance from the preceding phrase symbol is L1 morae or less, use the weak
symbol or omit it -- and L1 = 5 at about 7 morae/s, the rate this runs at.  With
the rule applied every ratio falls below 1.0.  The threshold is a property of
the synthesis model, not only of the text.

Single-word contours are bit-identical to before this work, with P0 off.


The nasals: murmur against oral locus
--------------------------------------

  50-ne-before-after.wav   neko, ane, onegai -- before, then after, per word
  51-n-row.wav             nana, nini, nunu, nene, nono
  52-m-before-after.wav    mame, mimi, momo -- the m row had the same error
  53-nasal-release.wav     neko, namae -- release frame, then without it
  54-nasal-words.wav       eight words

Reported: "the transition from N to E still has a stray phoneme, much like the
other transitions we've fixed."  It is the same error as the other four, in a
fifth place, and the file already carried a comment warning about it.

A nasal's MURMUR and its TRANSITIONS are two different things.  Fujimura's N2 of
about 1000 Hz is a resonance of the NASAL CAVITY, measured while the oral tract
is shut.  It describes the murmur and nothing else.  The formants that move into
and out of a nasal belong to the ORAL tract and are governed by where the oral
closure is -- alveolar for [n], the same place as /t/.  Taking the murmur's N2
as the transition's locus made /ne/ do this:

   F2  1004 -> 1172 -> 1348 -> 1524 -> 1700 -> 1876       over 50 ms

and that sweep through the middle of the F2 range is the extra segment.  The
same error ran the other way too: the /a/ of `ane` bent DOWN to 1004 before
jumping up, where an alveolar should pull it UP toward 1400.

A nasal shares its oral place with the homorganic stop, so the loci are taken
from those rows rather than invented: /n/ from /t/, /ny/ from /ky/, /my/ from
/py/.  Measured on the rendered audio by LPC, not off the parameter tracks:

   neko, before   886 837 823 842 771 904 1181 1392 1531 1711 1806 1891
   neko, after    886 837 823 842 793 1897 1807 1865 1875 1904 1887 1891

Frames spent in the 1300-1650 Hz band, which is where the stray segment lives:
two before, none after.

/m/ does NOT borrow the /p/ row.  Doing so gave /mo/ a start of 1780 against the
vowel's 856 -- a 924 Hz fall out of a bilabial, where locus theory has labials
raising F2 into a back vowel and barely moving.  Move size is diagnostic: a
locus should leave small transitions for most vowels, which /t/'s row duly does
for /n/.  LOCUS['p'] runs 1600-2250 and sits above the vowel for every vowel but
/i/, which is not how a labial behaves -- it looks like a burst-spectrum measure
rather than a formant locus, which would be this same category error again.  It
is flagged rather than rewritten on suspicion, and /m/ uses a labial locus of
750 Hz instead (the classic ~720 of locus theory, and consistent with LOCUS['w']
at 700-800, the labiovelar already in the table), attained halfway.  Every /m/
transition is now a rise, which is the labial pattern:

   /ma/ +237   /mi/ +658   /mu/ +271   /me/ +561   /mo/ +53

**The release is a step, and that is correct.** The murmur's resonances are the
nasal cavity's and the vowel's are the oral tract's; one does not glide into the
other, they swap.  What stops it being heard as a click in speech is that it
happens while the mouth is still nearly shut and the sound is weak.  So the step
is given a frame of its own at murmur amplitude, one frame before the amplitude
recovers, instead of landing on the same instant as it -- the two
discontinuities are separated rather than stacked.  Sample 53 is that frame
present and absent.

The closure, which is the mirror image, is deliberately left alone: there the
amplitude FALLS across the step and so masks it, where at the release it rises
and exposes it.  The physics is not symmetric even though the geometry is.

**The moraic /N/ before a vowel** is a different case and was also wrong.  It
stepped 870 Hz in one frame with no transition at all, because a vowel-initial
mora has no consonant and the transition loop was guarded on there being one.
Unlike [n], utterance-medial /N/ before a vowel is a nasalised vowel or uvular:
there is no closure to break, so the tongue moves continuously and a step is
simply wrong.  ten'in, zen'in, kin'en, hon'e all had it; hon'e now moves 176 Hz
a frame at most.

**Focus, from Kawai section 7.1.** His accent sandhi rules decide which of the
six accent symbols each prosodic word takes.  Example 7 settles the reading
past the OCR: six words, all accented, with -Emph on the fourth and sixth,
giving AH AM AM AL AM AL.  So only the FIRST accented word takes AH; later ones
take AM, or AL when suppressed.  This code gave every accented phrase AH.

+Emph does not raise the focused word so much as demote everything after it --
Kawai's rule changes every following 0Emph to -Emph -- which is how the focus
stands out.  With three accented phrases:

   neutral        AH AM AM     137.0  118.3  109.5
   +Emph on 1     AH AL AL     137.0  101.2   93.6
   +Emph on 2     AH AH AL     137.0  133.1   93.6
   +Emph on 3     AH AM AH     137.0  118.3  123.2

**And this retires DOWNSTEP.** The 0.80 multiplier was fitted to Kubozono's
Table 22 and only ever had his accented-to-accented case to answer to.  He
gives a second number, Table 23, for accented-to-heiban, and the fit got that
badly wrong.  Kawai's grading covers both from a rule:

                                accented->accented   accented->heiban
     Kubozono measured                 0.898               0.799
     fitted DOWNSTEP 0.80              0.871               0.904
     Kawai sandhi, no downstep         0.838               0.790

Mean absolute error halves, 0.066 to 0.035.  Stacking the multiplier on top of
the grading moves BOTH further off, so DOWNSTEP is 1.0 now.  Chaining over
three or more phrases still happens -- 137.0, 118.3, 109.5 -- through the decay
of the phrase component, which is where Fujisaki puts it rather than in the
command amplitudes.  The residual gap on the accented pair is speaker
difference: Kubozono's speakers want about 0.44 where Kawai's AM is 0.35, and
0.44 is not one of the six symbols.

**Questions, from Hirose section 4.** "The command for the interrogative
particle /ka/ is also assigned a large value (i.e. DH = 0.6), and its onset
timing is delayed by 70 msec as compared with the onset timing of the command
for the lexical accent."  0.6 is their DIALOGUE value -- they say the dialogue
rules raise every command, FH from 0.50 to 0.6 -- so a reader takes Kawai's AH.
It is an extra command on the final mora, on top of the phrase's own lexical
accent, and Hirose's reduced predicate does not apply because that rule is for
declaratives.  Over the final mora: ikimasu falls 141 -> 113, ikimasuka rises
113 -> 143, and the rise is held off for the 70 ms they specify.

**Prevoicing.** Japanese /b d g/ are prevoiced: the glottis keeps vibrating
into a shut tract and the low frequencies radiate through the walls.  This code
wrote track 0 = 0 for voiced and voiceless stops alike, so the only thing
separating /b/ from /p/ was VOT, where in Japanese prevoicing is the stronger
cue.  g + b + d are 11.12% of consonant onsets over all 486,646 pronounced
naist-jdic entries; the 8.76% this file used to print is their share of all
MORAE, a mislabelled denominator.  Measured off the audio, the
closure floor is now 44 dB against a vowel peak of 70 for /b d g/, and silence
for /p t k/.  Idemaru & Guion fold VOT into the closure and give no figure for
the bar's level, so the level is authored; what is categorical is that it is
there.

**The moraic nasal's place.** This was half done.  The MURMUR already went
bilabial before /p b m/, but the transition out of the preceding vowel was
keyed on 'n' whatever followed, so every moraic nasal pulled the vowel toward
the ALVEOLAR ridge -- including before /k g/, and including utterance-finally,
where Tokyo Japanese has no alveolar closure at all.  Now:

   kanpai  bilabial 988      sanka  velar 1530      sanda  alveolar 1400
   nihon, nippon, ten'in     no oral closure: the vowel keeps its own posture

That last case is the same reasoning as [h], which has no posture of its own
either: utterance-finally /N/ is a nasalised vowel or uvular, and inventing a
closure for it puts a segment there that is not.

**The labials.** Flagged last time as suspect but not rewritten on suspicion.
A second, independent method now agrees.  Fit a locus equation to each row --
attained = V + k(L - V), regressing the attained value on the vowel's own F2 --
and a real locus gives 0 < k <= 1 with a plausible L.  Run over the whole
inventory, /p/ and /b/ fit CLEANLY, k = 0.64, but at L = 2157 Hz, which is a
palatal place.  A bilabial locus is about 720.

The diagnostic also shows where NOT to act.  /k/ and /g/ come out at k = 0.17,
which looks wrong until you notice that a low k is exactly right for a velar --
the attained value tracks the vowel's own F2, which is the velar pinch -- so
the extrapolated L is meaningless there.  /sh ch j/ come out at k = 1.06, which
is just a fully attained constant, correct for a constriction they hold.

So only /p/ and /b/ move, and only their PLACE moves: rebuilt at the classic
labial locus of 750 with the k their own row fits.  /m/ was given a labial
locus last time at k = 0.5, authored; it now shares the measured 0.64, so the
three labials agree with each other and with their place.  Every labial
transition is a rise, which is the labial pattern:

   /pa/ +304   /pi/ +843   /pu/ +347   /pe/ +719   /po/ +68

Whatever Tanaka's row measures, using it to START A VOICED TRANSITION was the
error -- the same one as taking a fricative's frication F2 for its locus, or a
nasal's murmur N2 for its own.  That is the seventh instance of it.

**Phrase-initial /r/, from Arai.** "While phrase-initial /r/ is often produced
as a plosive-like sound."  Every /r/ here was the intervocalic flap wherever it
fell, including at the start of an utterance, where there is no preceding vowel
for the flap to interrupt: the intensity dip had nothing to dip into and the
first half of its transition did not exist.  His allophone list gives the
retroflex plosive for this position, so it is now a short voiced stop -- which
the prevoicing above fills, the two changes meeting.

Single-word contours are STILL bit-identical to before any prosody work, and
the inventory scan over 40,001 naist-jdic pronunciations is still complete.

Two papers and a released dataset, in jp_res/acoustics/vowels/.

**The first thing they do is confirm the vowel table.** Yazawa & Kondo (2019)
released the measurements behind their ICPhS paper: 16 speakers, 8 of each sex,

   /a/  687/1283  against  737/1225        /i/  301/2154  against  298/2067
   /u/  348/1435  against  356/1293        /e/  443/1947  against  481/1873
   /o/  462/ 949  against  456/ 856

F1 agrees within 50 Hz on every vowel and F2 within 142.  Two corpora, two

**Long vowels are more peripheral.** Hirata & Tsukada (2003): "the long vowels
occupied a more peripheral portion of the F1-F2 vowel space than the short
vowels did.  This supports a suggestion that long vowels resist coarticulation
to a greater extent than short vowels do."  A short vowel undershoots its
target; a long one has the time to reach it.  This code used ONE target for
both, so /o/ and /o:/ were the same vowel held for different lengths.

Their study is two speakers and they call it tentative, so it was checked
against Yazawa's sixteen.  It holds for four vowels of five:

   a  F1 687 -> 744, lower        i  F2 2154 -> 2293, fronter
   e  F2 1947 -> 2043, fronter    o  F2  949 ->  813, backer
   u  F2 1435 -> 1442, no move at all

/u/ not moving is itself right: Japanese /u/ is compressed and central and has
nowhere peripheral to go.

The two corpora disagree on ABSOLUTE values, so those are not swapped in.  A
WITHIN-corpus difference does transfer, so it is Yazawa's long-minus-short
delta that is applied to Mokhtari's targets.  Both morae of a long vowel take
the peripheral target, not just the held half, which needs a look at the next
mora because the ':' that marks it comes afterwards.  Measured back out of the
frames, all five land within 4 Hz of target: /a:/ 796/1180, /i:/ 304/2204,
/u:/ 360/1092, /e:/ 500/1972, /o:/ 448/724.

**And long vowels were a third too long.** The same dataset gives durations,
and this is where an error was hiding:

   short  mine 70 ms   measured 65.5-75.3   -7% to +7%
   long   mine 210 ms  measured 149.9-168.3  +25% to +40%

The short vowels were already right, so the whole error was in one number: the
':' mora was given a full MORA_FRAMES, which made the long/short ratio 3.00
where the measurement is 2.24.  LONG_FRAMES = 9 gives 2.29, within 2%.

That looks like it should fight mora isochrony, and it does not.  Mora timing
is approximate and the special morae are the known exception -- a long vowel's
second mora is not a full mora.  Over 1,481 dictionary words the mean rate goes
6.79 to 6.90 morae/s against the 7.0 the rules assume, so the change moves the
corpus CLOSER to the target rate while fixing the ratio.  Words with many
special morae do come out faster in morae/s -- toukyou, which is half special
morae, runs at 8.0 against konnichiwa's 6.5 -- and that is the expected
pattern, not a regression.

Their rate finding is not implemented and cannot be: short /e/ and /o/ are the
most susceptible to speaking rate and /i/ the least, but this synthesiser has
one rate.  It is the thing to apply first if a rate control is ever added.


Fricative against affricate: the 2015 paper re-read, plus the 2021 follow-up

Amano & Yamakawa (2021), "Mapping Japanese alveolar fricative, alveolar
affricate, and alveolo-palatal affricate in time-spectral space", is the
follow-up to the 2015 paper and it makes the structure explicit.  Two
ORTHOGONAL axes separate the four consonants:

  TIME       tells a fricative from an affricate.  /s/ and [C] group together.
  3,150 Hz   the mean intensity in a one-third-octave band tells an
             alveolo-palatal from an alveolar.  [C] and [tC] group together.

Their Table 1, two independent sets of 63 words each, agreeing closely:

                 rise      steady+decay    total     3,150 Hz band
     /s/        47.5 ms       87.1 ms     134.6 ms      39.3 dB
     /ts/       27.6          45.7         73.3         40.2
     [tC]       32.1          46.6         78.7         54.1

The varimax-rotated discriminant is F1 = -0.020 z1 - 0.219 z2 + 1.030 z3, which
is the 3,150 Hz band almost alone -- it is the whole of the place distinction.

**Checked on this synthesiser, the spectral axis was already right.**  SIB_POST
puts /s/ and /ts/ at 4000/4080 and [C], [tC] and /j/ at 3000/3700, which
straddles the band.  Measured on the rendered audio with a 2806-3536 Hz filter:

     [C] - /s/   +11.9 dB        [tC] - /ts/   +9.0 dB

against their +14.  Right sign, right ordering, somewhat smaller; absolute dB
across two different systems is not directly comparable, so the placement is
left alone -- it is also the one that was chosen by ear earlier and that matched
their published centre of gravity.

**The time axis was not right, and the reason is embarrassing.**  The base was
7 frames for /s/ and [C] against 5 and 6 for the affricates -- a ratio of 1.27,
where the entire point of both papers is about 1.8.  The finding had been
written into the comment above MANNER and never into the numbers underneath it.
10 frames gives 1.82 against their 1.77.  It costs 0.1 morae/s over 1,200
dictionary words, 6.90 to 6.80 against a target of 7.0.

These stay below the absolute measurements on purpose.  Their tokens are
citation-form and word-initial, where 134 ms of frication plus its vowel is far
more than one 140 ms mora.  What transfers between their context and connected
speech is the ratio, not the absolute -- the same reasoning as the long-vowel
delta.

**And a correction.** The old comment here said the 2015 durations were measured
before a DEVOICED /u/, and shortened the base on that basis.  They were not.
Both papers' selection criterion is word-initial before an UNDEVOICED -- that
is, a voiced -- /u/.  I read "undevoiced" as "devoiced".  The growth before a
genuinely devoiced vowel is therefore unmeasured by either paper, so FRIC_GROWN
is now sized to the mora instead: base 10 plus 4 fills one mora exactly.


Vowel to vowel
---------------

  68-hiatus-before-after.wav  aoi, ie, ue, iu, ai -- step, then glide
  69-hiatus-words.wav         nine words with vowel sequences
  70-n-before-vowel.wav       ten'in, zen'in, kin'en, hon'e

A vowel-initial mora got NO transition, because the loop that writes one keyed
on there being a consonant.  Where the mora before it also ended voiced -- a
vowel, a long vowel, or the moraic nasal -- that is simply wrong: nothing
interrupts the voicing, so the tongue travels continuously from one target to
the next and there is no boundary for a step to hide at.  `aoi` was three flat
plateaus:

   before   F2  1228 ... 1228 | 860 ... 860 | 2068 ... 2068     step 1208 Hz
   after    F2  1228 1132 1044 948 860 ... 860 1028 1204 1372
                1548 1724 1892 2068                             step  176 Hz

Japanese is full of these -- aoi, ie, ue, iu, ai, au, oi, kaeru, shiai, ookii,
omoshiroi -- so it was not a rare case.  Only a pause or a geminate's silence
licenses a step now.  The one large step left in `ten'in` is inside the silent
/t/ closure, from the closure posture to the burst posture, at amplitude zero.

**On the paper.** Lofqvist (2009), "Vowel-to-vowel coarticulation in Japanese:
The effect of consonant duration", JASA 125(2) 636-639, is about something
adjacent to this and it is worth being clear which.  It is an ARTICULATORY
study -- magnetometer tracking of tongue, lip and jaw in five speakers -- and
its sequences are V1-consonant-V2 throughout: /kami, kammi/, /kamee, kammee/,
/kema, kemma/, each pair differing only in the length of an intervening /m/.
It does not study two adjacent vowels, which is the case that was broken here.

Its result is also a negative one for the hypothesis it set out to test.  The
prediction was more coarticulation across a short consonant; that found "no
strong support".  What it found instead: "subjects modulate the speed of the
tongue movement between the two vowels, making it slower during the long than
during the short consonant."  The movement's magnitude stays constant and its
duration follows the consonant, so the speed is whatever covers the distance in
the time available.

That is the opposite strategy from `_glide_len`, which fixes the speed at about
200 Hz of F2 per frame and lets the duration follow the distance.  It is not
taken as a reason to change it, for two reasons.  His measurement is of the
tongue, and across a stop closure the tract is silent, so where the formants sit
during the closure is inaudible -- what is heard is the off-glide and the
on-glide, which is what this models.  And the slew limit exists because steps
were audible, which is a constraint on the synthesiser that his speakers did not
have.  His finding does support letting a transition fill the time available
rather than running at a fixed rate, which is what the hiatus glide does.

Closure durations from his stimuli, for the record: short 54-95 ms, long
119-165 ms, against Idemaru & Guion's 69 and 206.


Hiatus across a phrase boundary, where the vowels are the same
---------------------------------------------------------------

  71-hiatus-phrase.wav     his particle-vowel examples, before and after
  72-hiatus-types.wav      adjective-vowel and word-word

Kitazawa & Kiriyama (2004), "Acoustic and Prosodic Analysis of Japanese
Vowel-Vowel Hiatus with Laryngeal Effect", Interspeech/ICSLP, is the paper for
the case the previous section does NOT cover.  Theirs is hiatus between two
accentual phrases where the two vowels are the SAME -- ga-aru, wa-ame, to-omou,
te-ekizo, no-otaku -- which is very common in Japanese because a noun plus a
particle is the commonest phrase shape and many predicates begin with a vowel.

There is no formant movement there to glide, so the fix made for `aoi` does
nothing at all and the two vowels run together as one long one.  Measured
before: `wa ame` was a flat run of amplitude 60 straight through, with no
boundary cue of any kind.

Their 45 hiatus from the Japanese MULTEXT corpus, by EGG and spectrogram:

   17 clear glottalization, 23 weak, 5 phrase-final nasalization instead

so about 89% carry glottalization.  The worked example gives its size: the EGG
open quotient falls 80% -> 45% -> 60% and F0 falls with it, 235 -> 142 -> 178
Hz.  As ratios of the pre-boundary F0 that is 1.00 -> 0.60 -> 0.76, which is
what is used, over about five glottal periods.  Measured after, on `wa ame`:

   AV   60 60 60 60 56 52 56 60 60
   F0   93 93 93 56 63 70 86 85 84

Their second cue is durational: "the ratio of duration of vowel segment in the
preceding phrase-final position to the following phrase-initial position was
1.7 in average", with the pair totalling about two morae, so it redistributes
rather than adds.  Measured after: wa-ame and ga-aru both land on 1.70,
to-omou and te-ekizo on 1.60 because their first mora carries a consonant that
does not scale with the vowel.

"In cases the following word is emphasized ... the ratio becomes as low as
0.76."  He gives that as an example rather than a mean, and it is used as one;
it is wired to the same +Emph that drives Kawai's accent sandhi, so emphasis
now moves both the pitch and the durations.  Measured: 0.81 and 0.88.

**Two effects had to be made to compose rather than compete.** A vowel can be
both phrase-initial, which shortens it, and pre-geminate, which lengthens it --
`ni itte` is exactly that, and it is one of his own examples.  Both effects are
measured, so neither has a claim to override the other; the duration factors
multiply.  `ni itte` lands at 1.23, between the two.

**Not implemented, and worth knowing.** His third cue is phrase-final
NASALIZATION, and it is really a consequence of something else: the particle ga
is [Na] in Tokyo Japanese at non-word-initial position -- bidakuon -- so the
nasal murmur contrasts with the following non-nasal vowel.  Five of his 45 rely
on it alone and twelve more on it together with glottalization.  Implementing it
means giving /g/ a medial nasal allophone.  All /g/ onsets are 4.87% of
consonant onsets over the dictionary and the noninitial ones -- the only
position the allophone could apply in -- are 4.40%; those are positional counts
and neither says how often a speaker realises it nasally, which is the quantity
that would matter.  It is what Idemaru & Guion saw in their speakers.  It is
left out on purpose:
he notes his own speaker, "as a younger generation in the drift of phonological
change, tend[s] to pronounce these ga with non-nasals or at least weakly
nasalized", so it is a dialect and generation choice rather than a correction.


Kitazawa 2006: the same study, and it corrects the 2004 numbers
-----------------------------------------------------------------

  74-hiatus-cues.wav       ni|i and no|o, then wa|a and to|o

"Acoustic Features of Japanese Vowel-Vowel Hiatus at Prosodic Boundaries",
Speech Prosody 2006, is the follow-up to the 2004 paper on the same corpus, and
re-reading the two together changes the numbers taken from the first.

**The 40% F0 dip implemented from 2004 was the emphasized extreme.** That
figure comes from its Figure 1, which that paper itself describes as a phrase
"focused and emphasized" -- a detail present in the 2004 text and missed when
the dip was first built.  2006 adds the corpus statistics, and they are far
smaller.  Its Table 1 gives the F0 change at the glottalization point:

      hiatus     % decrease per period     % increase
      a | a            0.96                   3.25
      i | i            3.22                   1.70
      o | o            3.46                   5.12

"about 3% ... standard deviation 0.9%", and crucially "measured as differences
between the fundamental frequencies of the ADJACENT PERIODS" -- which is the
local discontinuity, not the depth of the whole dip.  The two reconcile: 3% a
period compounded over the five periods either side of the boundary that his
excising experiment spans is about 12%, and his 2006 worked example is a 22%
dip recovering to 96%.

The scale is now anchored on the two examples whose VOWEL is identified, one
per condition, rather than on an unlabelled one:

   2006 Fig. 3   "another a|a hiatus where F0 drops about 10 Hz"  ~4% at 250 Hz
   2004 Fig. 1   kichinto | okonawarenakatta, so o|o, and emphasized: 40%

Both are reproduced exactly.  The per-vowel figures from Table 1 scale the
depth, which is what makes a|a subtle -- and a|a is ga|a and wa|a, much the
commonest hiatus, of which he says in both papers that they "depict rather
vague features of glottalization if the following phrase is not emphasized":

                 neutral   emphasised
      a | a        4.0%       11.2%
      i | i       13.2%       37.0%
      o | o       14.3%       40.0%
      e | e       10.5%       29.4%     (no data; takes the mean)

A caveat on the a|a anchor, in his own figure caption: Fig. 3 is labelled
BI = 1, the WEAKEST of the five break indices.  So 4% is a floor for a|a rather
than its mean, and a|a at a stronger boundary should be deeper.  He publishes
no BI-by-depth table, so there is nothing to scale it with; noted, not guessed.

**Which cue marks the boundary follows from the particle.** His conclusion
groups them: "ga|a, ni|i, no|o" show the +/-nasal contrast, while "wa|a,
shika|a, te|e, to|o" are glottalized.  The split is not arbitrary -- the first
group is exactly the particles whose consonant is NASAL.  ni and no have /n/;
ga is [Na] in Tokyo Japanese.  So the vowel before the boundary is nasalized by
its own consonant and the one after it is not, and the contrast marks the
boundary without any need for glottalization.

The two mostly co-occur rather than compete -- of his 16 nasal-contrast cases,
4 used it alone and 12 had glottalization as well -- so nasalization is added
and nothing is suppressed.  Measured on the first formant bandwidth of the
phrase-final vowel against the phrase-initial one:

      ni itte   B1 122 -> 62 Hz        no otaku  B1 118 -> 58 Hz
      wa ame    B1 170 -> 170          to omou   B1  58 ->  58

ga|a falls into the glottal group here only because bidakuon is not
implemented, so /g/ is not nasal in this inventory.  That is the right
behaviour by accident rather than by design, and he notes his own speaker
barely nasalized ga anyway: "the spectrographic contrast is not very clear".

**His closing paragraph is addressed to this kind of work directly:**

   "some small abrupt discontinuity in vocal source generator is sharply sensed
   by our auditory system to effectively segment phrases, words, and phonemes.
   Accordingly, speech synthesizers may need to take much more care in their
   smoothness and discontinuity of the artificial vocal source generator so as
   to cause natural prosodic signs as well as to prevent unnecessary signs to
   confuse listeners."

Which is both halves of what this file has been doing: removing steps that mean
nothing -- the nasal release, the vowel hiatus, the murmur-as-locus transitions
-- while deliberately placing the ones that do, the flap's intensity dip and
this glottalization.


Stop bursts: Kitazawa & Doshita 1984
--------------------------------------

  75-t-burst.wav     ta te to tabemasu tokei -- flat, then rising
  76-ptk-bursts.wav  pa ta ka / pi ti ki / po to ko

  78-k-front.wav     /k/ before a front vowel, per-vowel table then 4.7 kHz

"Discrimination of Japanese voiceless stop consonants by burst spectrum", J.
Acoust. Soc. Japan.  28 male speakers, /p t k/ in CV, averaged critical-band
spectra to 9.25 kHz -- wider than the 5 kHz usually used, which turns out to
matter -- and vowel- and speaker-independent linear discriminants that classify
place correctly over 80% of the time, about 90% with the vowel known.  Their
Fig. 2(a) gives the gross shapes:

     [p]   flat, with little low-frequency energy
     [t]   high-rising, with its peak at 5.3 kHz
     [k]   compact: 1.2 kHz before back vowels, 4.7 kHz before front

**/t/ was flat here, and that was picking one arm of an either/or.** Morikawa
says the postdental closure "brings EITHER a flat spectrum OR increasing of
energy in the higher frequencies"; this took the first and attributed it to him,
which the comment above MANNER said in those words.  Kitazawa settles it on 28
speakers: rising, peak 5.3 kHz.

5.3 kHz is above the 4080 Hz this engine's F4 reaches, exactly as /s/'s cavity
is, so the burst cannot be made in the cascade at all -- the formants go to the
ceiling and the peak is carried by parallel band 6 with track 8.  Same place of
articulation as /s/, and reassuringly nearly the same peak.  Measured on the
rendered audio, over the burst frames only, with the ratio of energy above
4 kHz to energy below 2 kHz:

                peak Hz     hi/lo dB
     /pa/         467        -32.8        falling
     /ta/        4667        +32.9        rising      (was flat)
     /ka/        1380        -41.1        compact, back
     /ki/        2902        -10.1        compact, front
     /ko/         880        -41.8        compact, back

which is the three-way separation the paper's discriminant is built on.

**Two disagreements are left open rather than settled by me.**

/p/: Morikawa (270 utterances, pole-zero fit) has it diffuse-falling at a
constant -8 dB/octave on a real pole under 300 Hz.  Kitazawa (28 speakers,
critical-band) has it flat: "[p]'s spectrum is normally flat, and unless spoken
carefully the low-frequency energy is small, so cases that could be regarded as
the falling type of Blumstein et al.'s template were few."  He offers the
reconciliation himself -- falling needs low-frequency energy that only careful
speech has -- and Morikawa's material is read CV syllables, which is careful.
Both can be right of their own corpus.  Left on Morikawa; `P_FLAT` gives the
other, measured at peak 3767 and -9.6 dB against -32.8.

/k/ before front vowels: he reports 4.7 kHz, this table has 3100 for /i/ and
2100 for /e/.  His figure is a front/back summary of a spectrum averaged over
all speakers, where BURST_PEAK is per vowel from Morikawa, so they are not
quite the same quantity.  `K_FRONT = 4700` gives his, measured at 4012 and
+11.4 dB against 2902 and -10.1.  /k/ is the most frequent consonant in the
language, so this one is worth an ear before it moves.

**And a correction to something written here earlier.** When LOCUS['p'] was
rebuilt, the guess offered for why Tanaka's row could not be a locus was that
"it looks like a burst-spectrum measure".  That does not survive this paper.  A
labial burst spectrum is vowel-INDEPENDENT -- Morikawa says so outright, and
Kitazawa's entire result is a vowel- and speaker-independent discriminant --
while Tanaka's row varies by 650 Hz across the five vowels.  Whatever it
measures, it is not a burst spectrum.  The move-size evidence against using it
as a locus stands; the explanation offered for it was unsupported and is
withdrawn.


VOT: the one number that was off was /k/
------------------------------------------

Journal of Phonetics, PII S0095447006000120.  The article is paywalled and
returned HTTP 403, so these are the figures as the user reported them and not
read from the paper here: 13 monolingual Japanese speakers, word-initial stops,
mean VOT

     /p/ 30.0 ms      /t/ 28.5 ms      /k/ 56.7 ms

The paper's own point is that Japanese voiceless stops sit BETWEEN the
conventional short-lag and long-lag categories -- neither plainly unaspirated
nor plainly aspirated -- and that the long-running disagreement about which
they are does not resolve in either direction.  At 28-57 ms they are indeed
between a Spanish-like 10-20 and an English-like 60-80.

Checked against what was already here, /p/ and /t/ needed nothing: 3 frames
each, 30 ms, which is 0.0 and 1.5 ms off.  That matters beyond the two values,
because it says the scale is not inflated by their material being word-initial
the way Yamakawa's fricative durations are -- so the gap at /k/ is a real gap
and not a context difference.

/k/ was 5 frames, 50 ms, 6.7 ms short.  It is the value that carries the
finding: a velar VOT nearly twice the other two, which is the cross-linguistic
pattern of VOT lengthening as the constriction moves back.  6 frames is 3.3 ms
over instead of 6.7 under and makes the ratio 2.00 against their 1.89.  It
costs 0.05 morae/s over 1,200 dictionary words, from 6.84 to 6.79.

Measured back out of the frames, release to voicing onset:

     /pa/ 30 ms   /ta/ 30 ms   /ka/ 60 ms
     /po/ 30      /te/ 30      /ko/ 60

Not implemented from it: the "intermediate" character is carried by the VOT
values themselves, but there is a second dimension to it -- how strongly the
interval is aspirated, as against merely being a release transient before
voicing.  Every voiceless stop here aspirates through the cascade for the whole
VOT.  No number for aspiration intensity is available, so it is left alone
rather than guessed at.


Measuring the output the way the literature measures speech
-------------------------------------------------------------

Idemaru, Holt & Seltman (2012), JASA 132(6) 3950-3964, "Individual differences
in cue weights are stable across time: The case of Japanese stop lengths".
Two things come out of it: a segmentation procedure, and a pair of numbers that
turn out to say something about this synthesiser's whole timing model.

**The procedure**, which they credit to Peterson & Lehiste (1960), Klatt (1976),
Lahiri & Hankamer (1988), Hankamer et al. (1989) and Ham (2001):

   vowel     from the first complete cycle of periodic oscillation to the last
             complete cycle in the waveform
   closure   from the end of the last periodic cycle of the preceding vowel to
             the onset of the stop burst
   VOT       from the onset of the visible burst to the onset of the first
             complete periodic cycle in the following vowel

Every duration reported in this file until now came off the PARAMETER TRACKS,
which is the easy measurement and not the one any paper makes.  measure.py
implements the above against the rendered waveform.  It is partly validated:

   /apa/   closure 38 ms, VOT 30 -- against 30 on the tracks and 30.0 measured
   /aka/   closure 34 ms, VOT 68 -- against 60 on the tracks and 56.7 measured
   /ata/   fails to segment

Three-way agreement on /p/, 8-11 ms high on /k/, and /t/ not working.  The
reason /t/ fails is worth recording: its burst is now broadband noise carried on
parallel band 6, and the 32 ms analysis window needed to track a 90 Hz pitch
period is coarse against a 10 ms boundary, so the window straddles burst and
vowel.  Two bugs were found and fixed on the way -- a normalised
autocorrelation computed over near-silence reads near 1, so silent closures
measured as fully voiced; and taking the best of ~227 candidate lags lets
broadband noise score 0.5-0.7 by chance, which needed a zero-crossing gate.

**The numbers, and what they say.** They cite Idemaru & Guion-Anderson (2010)
for singleton closure 78 ms and geminate 225 ms, and for the RELATIVE measure,
closure divided by the preceding syllable, singleton 0.42 and geminate 1.08.
Their paper is about those two cues being nearly equally informative for the
Japanese length contrast, and about listeners differing individually in which
they weight.  Measured here:

                        this synthesiser      measured
     singleton closure       50 ms               78          -36%
     geminate closure       190 ms              225          -16%
     relative, singleton      0.36                0.42        -14%
     relative, geminate       1.06                1.08         -2%

**The absolute durations run short and the relative ones are nearly exact.**
That is not a coincidence: the mora budget compresses absolute durations while
leaving ratios intact, because a consonant takes what it needs and the vowel
gets the rest of the same 140 ms.  It is the same shape of result as the
fricative durations and the long vowels -- the ratio transfers between their
material and connected speech, the absolute does not.

Taking 78 ms at face value was tested and rejected: 8 frames of closure makes a
/ka/ mora 200 ms against a nominal 140, a 43% overrun on one mora, and drops
the corpus rate from 6.78 to 6.58 morae/s.  The measurement is right of its own
material and will not fit a 7 morae/s mora, so it is not taken.

What this means in their terms is worth stating plainly: a listener who weights
RELATIVE duration is served well by this synthesiser, and one who weights
ABSOLUTE duration is given a contrast about 30% weaker than a real talker's.
Their finding is that both kinds of listener exist in roughly equal numbers.


Homma 1981: duration is a WORD-level relationship
---------------------------------------------------

Yayoi Homma, "Durational relationship between Japanese stops and vowels",
Journal of Phonetics 9 (1981) 273-281.  Four speakers, 24 two- and three-mora
/CaCa/ and /CaCCa/ words in the frame "Kore wa ___ desu", closure duration, VOT
and vowel duration measured for each.  This is the paper that the duration
problems recorded above were waiting on, and it supplies three things.

**VOT depends on POSITION IN THE WORD, and strongly.** Her Table III:

      initial   /p/ 24-29   /t/ 32   /k/ 45-61      voiceless mean 37 ms
      medial    /p/  7      /t/ 16   /k/ 24         voiceless mean 16 ms

One VOT per consonant was used here regardless of position, taken from
word-INITIAL measurements, so every medial stop ran two to four times long --
and medial is much the commoner position.  "Gemination of stops did not affect
VOT" (/pp/ 11, /tt/ 13, /kk/ 28, mean 17), so geminates take the medial figure
too.  Measured back out of the frames afterwards:

                 initial   Homma      medial   Homma
      /p/          30        24          10       7
      /t/          30        32          20      16
      /k/          60        45          20      24

all within 6 ms, and the position effect is now there where before it was not.

Finding that needed a bug fixed first.  The burst loop had `max(2, vot // 2)`,
a two-frame floor, which silently overrode any VOT below 20 ms -- so medial /p/
at 7 ms came out 20, identical to /t/ and /k/, and the whole position effect
was invisible in the output even once the table was right.

**The voiced/voiceless closure contrast was less than half what she measures.**
Her Table II, medial single stops: voiceless 67 ms, voiced 44.  This had 50
against 40.  Now 70 against 40, measured.

**And the compensation is a word-level effect, not a mora-level one.** Her
worked example, which is the whole paper in six numbers:

                 VOT  vowel  closure  vowel    word
       p a p a    18    67     77      98     260 ms
       g a g a    13   109     40     105     267 ms

"In spite of the big difference in the first syllable duration, the two words
are almost the same in the word duration.  This means that temporal
compensation works within a word, not within a syllable or mora."

Her Table VI also gives the vowel after a voiceless stop as 77 ms against 105
after a voiced one, and that was briefly applied here as a separate multiplier
on the vowel.  It is not a separate fact.  It is what the closure contrast
PRODUCES inside a fixed budget, and applying both double-counted: /gaga/ came
out 320 ms against /papa/ 280, because a multiplier lengthens the word instead
of redistributing within it -- which is the exact error her paper is about.
Widening the closure contrast and leaving the vowel as the remainder gives her
compensation for free:

       papa 280 ms   gaga 280 ms       against her 260 and 267

**What is fixed and what is not.** The stop half of the duration problem
recorded earlier is largely gone: the corpus rate is 6.91 morae/s against the
7.0 the rules assume, the closest yet, and words with no overrunning consonant
still land on n x 140.  But the word-duration inflation is not gone, it has
changed owner.  It is now carried by the FRICATIVES -- /s/, /sh/, /ch/, /ts/,
/j/ -- which still take 90-100 ms of frication from Yamakawa's ratio and have
no position-dependent shortening because no source here measures one.  Steps of
+13 to +33 ms per such consonant remain.

Closing that needs the two-pass restructure her paper actually implies: compute
every mora's requirement first, set the word's budget from its mora count, and
distribute the remainder across the vowels.  Her Table VIII supports the
budget -- 2-mora words 268 ms, 3-mora 383, a ratio of 2.9 against the
linguistic 3, and 134 and 128 ms per mora respectively against the 140 used
here.  That is the next piece of work on timing, and it is architectural rather
than another number.


The build restructured: a word budget
---------------------------------------

Homma's conclusion is architectural, not another number: "temporal compensation
works within a word, not within a syllable or mora."  Until now this built each
mora independently -- `total = max(MORA_FRAMES, need)` -- so a consonant that
overran its share simply made the word longer, and nothing anywhere gave the
time back.  Measured over 1,500 dictionary words, every overrunning consonant
added 13 to 33 ms with no compensation at all.

**How it works now.** Each mora records where it starts, which stretch of it is
plain steady vowel and so adjustable, and what it is nominally worth.  After
the whole word is built, `_compensate` takes the difference between the sum of
the nominal values and what was actually emitted and spends it across the
adjustable runs in proportion to the room each has.

The nominal budget is NOT the mora count times 140 ms.  It is the sum of each
mora's own nominal length, because the special morae are deliberately not 140:
a long vowel's second mora is 90 ms from Yazawa's 2.24 ratio, and a pause is
whatever Kawai's symbol says.  Summing the nominals preserves all of that and
compensates only the ordinary morae against each other.

Three things are budgeted but never spent, and each for a reason:

   the geminate closure   it IS the length cue; compensating there would eat
                          the contrast it exists to carry
   a pause                Kawai's symbol sets it, not the word's arithmetic
   a glottalised vowel's  its first frames carry the creak dip, and removing
   first frames           them would remove the boundary cue

A floor of two frames of steady vowel is never spent either, so a word whose
consonants genuinely will not fit still overruns rather than losing its vowels
-- `desu`, whose devoiced /su/ needs 20 frames against a 14-frame mora, comes
out 290 ms against a 280 budget, and that is correct.

**What it bought.** Word duration by number of overrunning consonants, over the
same 1,500 words:

                          before                    after
   3 morae (ideal 420)   417 443 465  (+26 +23)   413 415 420  (+2 +5)
   4 morae (ideal 560)   553 578 609 622          547 550 553 560  (+3 +3 +7)
   5 morae (ideal 700)   704 719 752 777          691 690 695 698  (-1 +5 +3)

From 13-33 ms of inflation per consonant to under 7.  Homma's own pair comes
out exactly equal -- /papa/ and /gaga/ both 280 ms, against her 260 and 267 --
where before the restructure they were 280 and 320.

The corpus rate moves from 6.91 to 7.27 morae/s, because words that used to
inflate no longer do.  That is above the 7.0 Kawai's rules assume but it is not
wrong: Homma's own Table VIII is 134 ms per mora for two-mora words and 128 for
three, which is 7.46 and 7.84 morae/s, and Hirose's reading-style speaker YY --
whose baseline F0 of 73.0 Hz this voice already matches at 72.0 -- read at 7.3.

Everything that had to survive the restructure did.  Measured after:

   geminate closure   ata 70 ms / atta 210, a ratio of 3.0 against Homma's 1:3
                      (it was 3.8 before her closure figures went in)
   Homma's pair       papa 280 / gaga 280
   long/short vowel   150 / 70 = 2.14 against Yazawa's 2.24
   the geminate F0 fall lands inside the word in every case, which it had to
   be remapped to do, since compensation moves every absolute frame index

Single-word F0 contours are still bit-identical on the frames they are given,
and the inventory scan over 40,001 pronunciations is still complete.


/d/ against /t/, and against /r/

**The paper is about the neighbouring contrast.** Liu Chiu-Yen (2002), "Recognition

                  closure   burst        amplitude dip
       /ara/        0 ms    none         14.4 dB
       /ada/       40 ms    yes          26.6 dB
       /ata/       70 ms    yes          silent

The stray phoneme after /t/, and Kochetov on voiced obstruents
----------------------------------------------------------------

Reported: "a small stray phoneme when it transitions between Ts and a vowel."
Measured on `ata`, the third formant ran

     2272 2272 ... 2272 | 4000 3136 | 2272 2272

a 1728 Hz excursion up and back over 20 ms, right at the release.

That is the burst posture.  /t/ and /d/ have F3 and F4 pinned at the 4000/4080
ceiling purely to place the 5.3 kHz band-6 noise Kitazawa & Doshita measured --
it is a noise-shaping device, exactly like SIB_POST -- and the post-burst
frames were gliding out of it into the vowel as though it were a vocal-tract
state.  That is the same mistake as starting a transition from a fricative's
frication F2 or a nasal's murmur N2, which this file warns about twice, and it
was introduced here two changes ago.  Eighth instance of the one error.

For those two the post-burst frames now go straight to the locus; the step from
the burst to it lands on a noise frame, where it is masked, exactly as the
nasal release step is.

**Kochetov (2014)** then fixed the rest of it.  "Voicing and Tongue-Palate
Contact Differences in Japanese Obstruents", J. Phonetic Society of Japan
18(2) 63-76: 64-electrode electropalatography, five speakers, voiced and
voiceless stops and fricatives between vowels.  His result is a crossover --
for stops the contact area is SMALLER for voiced than voiceless, for fricatives
it is the reverse, wider with a narrower central groove -- which he reads as
the two manners achieving voicing by different articulatory means.

The number that matters here: "the alveolar closure was on average wider for
/t/ than for /d/ (more than 2 rows vs. less than 1 row)", while "among the
velars, closure width was slightly higher for /k/ than for /g/ (both were less
than 1 row)".  Before high vowels /d/'s closure "was often absent altogether",
and one speaker lacked a complete closure at all, which he reads as lenition.

So /d/ no longer shares /t/'s locus row and /g/ no longer shares /k/'s.  A
constriction less than half as long cannot pull the formants as far, so /d/
sits halfway between /t/'s attained values and the vowel's own F2, and /g/ a
sixth of the way from /k/'s.  Mean displacement from the vowel:

     /t/ 203 Hz   /d/ 102      /k/ 299 Hz   /g/ 254      /p/ 456   /b/ 456

/b/ is unchanged on purpose: a labial makes no tongue-palate contact, so his
method has nothing to say about it.

**Three more steps came out of chasing this one**, all of them voiced-only,
because a voiced stop is voiced throughout where a voiceless one has a silent
closure to hide a posture change behind:

   the prevoicing bar took the ONSET posture, keyed on the following vowel,
   while the vowel before it had bent toward the CODA posture, keyed on the
   preceding one -- `age` jumped F2 624 Hz between them.  It takes the coda
   posture now, and glides across the closure rather than sitting still.

   the burst posture was as sharp for a voiced stop as a voiceless one, which

...and the stray phoneme after /t/ again, properly this time
-------------------------------------------------------------

Reported a second time, after the fix above.  The fix above was real but it was
not the thing being heard: it corrected formant postures, and the measurement
used to check it looked only at steps between VOICED frames, which stepped
straight over the release.

The release itself was the problem.  Counting what source is on each frame:

                     sibilant noise     aspiration
       /ta/              30 ms             0 ms
       /tsu/             70 ms            50 ms       the affricate
       /su/             140 ms            50 ms       the fricative
       /ka/               0 ms            60 ms
       /pa/               0 ms            30 ms

/ta/ had thirty milliseconds of /s/-type sibilant noise and no aspiration at
all -- 43% of the frication of the affricate /ts/, at a similar level.  That is
heard as a brief [s]: /ta/ was being said [tsa].  /k/ and /p/ were untouched
and aspirate normally, which is why only /t/ was affected.

The cause is a over-reach when Kitazawa & Doshita's burst went in.  They measure
the BURST spectrum -- Morikawa takes it over the first 12.8 ms -- and the high
alveolar source belongs to that transient alone.  The aspiration that follows is
glottal noise shaped by the tract, a different thing.  The sibilant source had
been put on every frame of the VOT.

Now the burst is one frame, about the 12.8 ms it is measured over, and the rest
of the VOT is aspiration:

       [5] burst       b6:66 n8:40   F3 4000
       [6] aspiration  asp:80        F3 2272
       [7] aspiration  asp:80        F3 2272
       [8] voicing

Sibilant noise in /ta/ drops from 30 ms to 10, from 43% of the affricate's
frication to 14%.  The burst keeps everything it was given: measured on the
burst frame alone it peaks at 4600 Hz and rises 38.5 dB from the low band,
which is Kitazawa's [t] exactly, against /ka/ compact at 1317 and /pa/ falling.

**Two lessons, both about the measurement rather than the code.** The first fix
was checked with a metric that excluded the frames where the fault was -- a
voiced-frame formant step cannot see a noise source.  And a borrowed
measurement carries its own scope: theirs was of a 12.8 ms transient, and
applying it to a 30 ms interval made a burst into a fricative.

/f/ = [P]: one phoneme, four sounds
-------------------------------------

  84-phi-four-ways.wav     [f] / [P] / [P/h] / [h] pinned onto fuku + fune

Scott Ruddell, "An acoustic study of the Japanese voiceless bilabial
fricative", San Francisco State University.  Ten native speakers, /f/ before all
five vowels in loan, Sino-Japanese and native words, read inside the carrier
"sore wa ___ desu"; the word-initial consonant identified by ear and then
checked against the spectrogram, with amplitude binned over 1-7, 7-13 and
13-20 kHz.

His conclusion is partly negative and is quoted rather than hidden: "there can
be no concise label of the phonetic realization of /f/."  FOUR sounds came back
where he expected two -- [f], [P], [h], and a blend he had to give its own
category, [P/h].  So /f/ does not get a posture here.  It gets four, and a rule.

What is not negative is the distribution.  His Fig. 3 sorts his word list by
which sound was heard, and the sort comes out systematic:

                              Ruddell's modal      chosen here
   futon, futarusan, fushigi        [P]                [P]      /fu/ + alveolar
   fuku, fukadakku                  [P/h]              [P/h]    /fu/ + velar
   fune, fuan                       [h]                [h]      /fu/ + nasal, vowel
   fuufu                       [P/h] and [h] tie       [h]
   fan, firumu, feruto, forio   [f] and [P]            [P]      loan /fa fi fe fo/

[f] is reachable only by pinning it.  He calls it "an irregularity of Japanese
... due to linguistic overlap caused by bilingualism" and finds it from the
speakers with the most English, so a Japanese voice should not use it; it is in
the table to be heard in 84, not to be chosen.

Replayed over all 486,646 pronounced naist-jdic entries the rule gives [P]
53.8%, [h] 23.1%, [P/h] 23.1%.  This file used to print 55/24/21, which
understated [P/h] by two points.  It is rule output on dictionary readings, not
observed speaker allophones, and it is not routed through the NJD pipeline.

The finding this is really built on is not a level.  It is a SHAPE IN TIME.
Figs. 7 and 9 are the same fricative with and without it: a prominent band at
1.7-1.8 kHz, which he reads as lip rounding, is missing from the first part of
[P] and present from the first instant of [P/h].  He counts it in Fig. 10 and it
is nearly categorical -- 33 of 36 [P] tokens show the onset with no band, 31 of
33 [P/h] tokens show it with one.  He calls the cause "initial lip closure": in
[P] the lips are still travelling when the noise starts, in [P/h] they are
already set.

A constriction that is still closing damps its own resonance, so the band is
broad at first and sharpens as the lips arrive.  That is a BANDWIDTH, which is
the one way to render this without moving F2 -- and F2 must not move here, or
the initial lip closure would be heard as a formant transition, which is to say
as a stray phoneme, which is the defect this project has had to take back out
eight times.  B2 runs 400 -> 120 Hz over the same 40% rise that Yamakawa &
Amano's amplitude trapezoid already uses, on the grounds that the lips arriving
and the turbulence reaching level are one gesture.  Measured on the rendered
audio, as dB of 1450-1950 Hz over the 700-1300 and 2200-3200 Hz skirts:

   frame            0     1     2     3     4     5
   [P]            0.0   9.5  12.6  12.8   9.0  14.1    the band comes in
   [P/h]          0.0  14.3  15.5  14.2   9.0  14.1    present from frame 1
   [h]            0.0  14.0  15.4  14.0   8.9  14.6
   [f]            0.0  14.2  15.2  13.8   8.4  13.3

Levels.  He reports amplitude not in dB but as the share of tokens in four bins
-- Prominent >=20 dB, Moderate 10-20, Weak 1-10, None <=0 -- because he could
not hold mic distance constant and says so.  Taking each bin at its midpoint
(22 / 15 / 5.5 / 0) and averaging his 1-7 kHz column, the only one of his three
bands that fits under this synthesiser's 8 kHz Nyquist at all, gives [f] 10.8
dB, [P/h] 9.3, [P] 9.0, [h] 4.6.  That reproduces his prose as a check -- he
says [P] and [P/h] are "very similar in the overall level of amplitude" and that
[h] "by far had the lowest amount of overall frication" -- so the derived
numbers are used as offsets from [P] at the engine's 80.

The band does NOT go in LOCUS, and this is the ninth time that distinction has
decided something.  1.7 kHz is measured IN THE NOISE; a bilabial locus is about
750.  Seeding the vowel from 1700 would start it ~750 Hz above where a labial
actually leaves it -- the same error already corrected for /h/, for /s/ and for
the nasal murmurs.  The band lives in a noise posture; LOCUS['f'] instead takes
the sourced labial row that /p/ and /b/ use, replacing the estimate that was
there.  Through the /f/ onset that is 64 Hz per frame at worst, against 192 Hz
for the synthesiser as a whole.

Three things the paper does not establish, and that are not claimed here:

  * anything about intervocalic /f/.  "Due to the limited scope of this paper,
    intervocalic /f/ sounds were not observed."  Every token is word-initial.
  * a band frequency for /fa fi fe fo/.  From his 4.2 onward he looks only at
    /fu/ -- "since the most significant variation occurred before /u/, from
    this point on I will focus on the fricatives in their /u/ environment" --
    and he ties the band to rounding for a following /u/.  The other four
    vowels take the vowel's own F2 instead: a bilabial slit has no front cavity
    of its own, so the noise is excited into a tract already shaped for the
    vowel.  Using 1700 there would push the noise 475 Hz ABOVE the vowel for
    /fa/, where a lip constriction can only pull F2 down.
  * absolute levels, for the reason above.

One claim is deliberately not applied as written.  Saito Yoshio (2003), cited
in Watanabe (2009) and quoted in Ruddell's 2.6: "[P] always appears when
followed by a voiceless vowel."  Read as "devoicing forces [P]" it erases the
velar rule, because /fu/ before a velar is nearly always a devoicing
environment -- and /fuku/ is exactly that, and Ruddell's own modal answer there
is [P/h].  What he writes is that "[P] and [P/h] occurred more often in
environments which vowel devoicing occurred", so devoicing argues against [h],
not against [P/h].  It is applied that way: it blocks [h] and does nothing
else.  The case it decides is word-final /fu/, as in toufu, where the vowel is
voiceless and the frication is the whole mora; [h] there would leave almost
nothing to hear.  Ruddell has no word-final /fu/ to check it against.


A devoicing bug, found while checking the above
-------------------------------------------------

Chasing why /fuan/ was picking [P] rather than [h] turned up a rule that has
nothing to do with /f/.  High vowels devoice between voiceless consonants or
finally after one, and the test was:

    nxt_c = M.split(morae[n + 1])[0] if n + 1 < len(morae) else None
    devoiced = (v in 'iu' and c in VOICELESS and
                (nxt_c in VOICELESS or nxt_c is None or nxt_c == ''))

M.split('a') is ('', 'a'), so the `nxt_c == ''` arm fired on every mora that
begins with a BARE VOWEL -- which is not a devoicing environment at all.  It
was presumably meant to catch a phrase boundary, but M.split(' ') is (' ', ''),
so boundaries never reached it and bare vowels always did.  /fuan/ devoiced its
/u/, and so did /shiai/ and /kuiru/ theirs.  Boundaries are now listed
explicitly, which also makes a phrase-final high vowel devoice, as "finally
after one" always intended.

Over 20,000 dictionary pronunciations: 8,069 morae devoiced under the old test,
7,270 under the fixed one.  799 of them -- 9.9% of every devoicing decision the
synthesiser made -- were spurious, touching 4.0% of words.  The corpus rate is
unmoved at 7.35 morae/s, and all 725 consonant-vowel-context combinations still
synthesise.


Sweeping for the next stray phoneme instead of waiting to hear it
-------------------------------------------------------------------

  89-voiced-sibilants.wav  aza mizu kazoku zenbu aji jikan jouzu kanji, paired
  90-s-z-sh-j.wav          asa aza / asha aji / suzu shiji / kasa kaza
  91-f3-bridge.wav         iwa uri iyu kiwa ariga oyu kuwa tori, paired

Every audible defect fixed in this Japanese work was found the same way: the
user heard something wrong, said so, and it turned out that a number measured
in NOISE -- a fricative's frication F2, a nasal's murmur N2, a burst's spectral
peak, a flap's contact posture -- had been used as a vocal-tract state and so
had become the start or the end of a VOICED transition.  Eight of those, one at
a time, each after the fact.

The defect has a signature, so sweep.py looks for it instead: a formant step
between two consecutive VOICED frames larger than a tract can make in 10 ms.
Fant's figure for the fastest formant movement is of the order of 1-2 kHz per
100 ms, so ~200 Hz per frame is the ceiling.  Two steps are deliberate and
sourced, and are excluded by name -- the nasal release, where the murmur's
nasal-cavity resonances and the vowel's oral ones swap rather than glide, and
the flap's contact, which Arai measures as instantaneous.

The sweep runs the 99 real morae in all five preceding-vowel contexts.  "Real"
matters: the first version took the cross product of consonants and vowels and
duly flagged /zi/, /yi/, /wi/, /ryi/ and /ti/, none of which Japanese has, and
sent me looking at defects in sounds that are never synthesised.  The inventory
is now read back from the kana table and the onset list, because M.to_morae is
a romaji reader and will accept any consonant in front of any vowel.

It found two more, neither of which anyone had reported.

THE VOICED SIBILANTS.  SIB_POST pushes F3 and F4 to the engine's ceiling to
place a sibilant peak the cascade cannot otherwise reach, and noise_post's own
docstring says those values "are a device for shaping noise, not a vocal-tract
state ... they must never start a voiced transition".  The docstring is right.
The code obeyed it only by accident: every consonant using that table was
voiceless, and with the voicing off, F3 at 4000 Hz shapes noise and nothing
else.  /z/ and /j/ are voiced and carry track 0 at 40 right through their
frication, so on those frames a 4000 Hz F3 is a resonance the VOICE is driving.
/uza/ ran F3 2224 -> 4000 -> 2272 across five voiced frames: 1776 Hz up, 1728
back.  It also measured +20.8 dB of 4 kHz over 2 kHz against /s/'s +14.4, so
the VOICED fricative was the brighter of the pair, which is backwards -- voiced
frication is the weaker of the two by a wide margin.

The fix is not simply to drop the trick.  Band 5 rides F3 and band 6 rides F4,
so abandoning both and leaning on track 8 -- the one noise source not tied to a
formant -- was tried first and put /z/'s peak at 8000 Hz, hard against the
Nyquist edge, because unshaped track 8 just rises to the top of the band.  /z/
is /s/'s voiced partner and has to peak near it.  So band 5 goes and band 6
stays: F3 comes back to the tract, F4 stays at the ceiling.  F4 is the weakest
and highest of the four poles and the vowels' own F4 is at 3282-3455 already,
so moving it to the ceiling is an ~800 Hz change to a pole that is nearly
there, where F3 was a 1776 Hz change to one that carries place.

F2 went the same way.  VOICED_F2 already exists for exactly this -- the F2 a
voiced transition may start from, where LOCUS holds a frication measurement --
and onset() and coda() both consult it.  noise_post did not.

   /z/ and /j/ frication          before      after      /s/, /sh/ for scale

   worst voiced F3 step, /z/      1776 Hz    368 Hz
   worst voiced F3 step, /j/       784 Hz      - Hz
   noise peak, /z/                4307 Hz   4480 Hz    /s/  4746 Hz
   noise peak, /j/                4078 Hz   4078 Hz    /sh/ 4102 Hz
   HF energy, /z/                  124.8 dB  119.4 dB  /s/  122.9 dB
   HF energy, /j/                  121.3 dB  119.8 dB  /sh/ 122.3 dB

/z/ was 1.9 dB louder than /s/ in frication and is now 3.5 dB quieter, which is
the direction voicing moves it.

THE F3 BRIDGE.  LOCUS is an F2 table; it says nothing about F3 or F4.  So
onset() takes those two from the FOLLOWING vowel and coda() from the PRECEDING
one, and where the two vowels differ they disagree at the consonant's first
frame.  Behind a stop's silent closure that is invisible.  A glide, a flap or a
voiced fricative is voiced and continuous, so it lands as a step: /iwa/ 672 Hz,
/uri/ and /iyu/ and /iryu/ 720.

Which vowel is right?  Neither -- the consonant has no F3 of its own to be
right about, which is why the table omits it.  The midpoint of the two flanking
vowels splits the move in half and hands each half to the offglide and the
transition, which already exist and already interpolate all seven values.  The
frication frames need it applied twice, because noise_post rebuilds the posture
from scratch and the bridge does not otherwise reach them.

   sweep.py, worst voiced-to-voiced step per consonant, Hz per 10 ms frame

                 before   after
      /z/         1776     368
      /j/          784       -
      /y/          720       -
      /r/          720     248
      /ry/         720       -
      /w/          672     304

What is left is 248-368 Hz, at stop releases and glide onsets.  A stop release
opens the mouth about as fast as anything in speech and a glide is a movement
by definition, so those are left alone rather than smoothed into something
slower than the articulation they stand for.  The corpus rate is unmoved at
7.35 morae/s and all 15 generators still run.

What this is, and what it is not.  A sweep over one acoustic signature, not a
listening test.  It looks for a formant step between two consecutive VOICED
frames larger than a tract can move in 10 ms, and it will not catch anything of
a different shape -- wrong durations, wrong amplitudes, wrong pitch, or a
phoneme that is simply the wrong sound.  Nor anything in a position it does not
test: every context is VCV with five flanking vowels, so nothing here exercises
clusters, phrase boundaries or long utterances.  And 200 Hz a frame is a round
number taken from Fant's order of magnitude, not a threshold measured for
Japanese.

It also excludes three things BY NAME, and if any of those three arguments is
wrong the sweep is silently blind there.  The nasal release and the flap
contact are argued from the source papers.  The prevoiced closure is argued
from a measurement -- 99.0% of its energy below 500 Hz against 0.6% between 1
and 3 kHz -- which is the stronger of the two kinds of argument.

It found two real faults that eight rounds of listening had not.  That is the
case for running it; none of the above is an argument against it.


Smoothing the rest, and being wrong about why they were there
---------------------------------------------------------------

  92-smooth-vc.wav     iwa aga ari igo kawa kagami tori nagai, paired
  93-smooth-words.wav  arigatou ohayou sayounara tomodachi ... paired
  94-sentences.wav     five utterances with phrase boundaries marked

The section above left 248-368 Hz standing at stop releases and glide onsets
and argued they should stay: a stop release opens the mouth about as fast as
anything in speech, a glide is a movement by definition, and slowing either
would make the synthesiser gentler than the articulation it stands for.

That argument was about the wrong thing.  Not one of the three was an
articulation moving fast.  All three were two parts of the code disagreeing
about where a formant was going, with the disagreement landing in one frame.

ONE.  The offglide never arrived.  _offglide interpolated from the vowel to the
consonant's posture with (i+1)/(n+1), so its last frame sat a full step short of
the target no matter how many frames it was given -- the move was always one
step incomplete, and that remainder was itself a step.  About 150 Hz of /iwa/'s
304.  Dividing by n lands it on the target.

TWO.  The offglide was aimed where the consonant does not sit.  coda() keys F2
on the PRECEDING vowel and onset() on the following one.  For a stop that
disagreement is correct and is Kashino's whole point: there is a closure between
the two, the articulator really does travel through it, and the burst at the far
end is keyed to the vowel after.  A glide, a flap or a fricative has no closure
to travel through -- it is one gesture with one posture -- so the vowel should
aim at the posture the consonant actually holds.  /iwa/ aimed at 1013 and held
887.

THREE.  F1 was aimed at an open tract and landed on a shut one.  A prevoiced
closure forces F1 to 200 through voice_bar(), but coda() aimed the vowel's F1 at
480, so F1 glided down to about 540 and then fell 344 Hz in a single frame as
the voice bar took over.  F1 is not a place cue; it tracks how open the tract
is, and the tract is shut.  Aim it at 200.

With those three fixed every consonant in the inventory sat at 208-248 Hz,
which is suspiciously flat, and it was a rounding.  _glide_len sized a
transition with int(d/SLEW + 0.5), so SLEW was a move's AVERAGE speed and not
its ceiling: a move worth 1.2 frames got one frame and ran at 240 Hz.  Rounding
up makes SLEW mean what the comment next to it always said it meant.  (The
reason this only surfaced now is fix ONE: while the glide stopped a step short,
each step was d/(n+1) and the rounding had slack to hide in.)

   sweep.py, worst voiced-to-voiced step, Hz per 10 ms frame

      before the voiced-sibilant work        1776
      after it                                368
      after this                              200   = SLEW, every consonant

One step is left and is deliberately not smoothed.  A prevoiced stop's closure
runs its formants from the preceding vowel's locus to the following one's --
/igo/ is the velar pinch, the place cue Kashino measures -- at about 240 Hz a
frame.  None of it is radiated.  voice_bar() sets F1 to 200 and all three
bandwidths to 250, and the rendered closure measures 99.0% of its energy below
500 Hz against 0.6% between 1 and 3 kHz, 9.3 dB down on the vowel.  The move is
bookkeeping for where the tongue is, not a transition anyone hears, and
smoothing it would spend closure duration -- which Homma measured -- on an
inaudible number.  sweep.py skips voice-bar frames by name for that reason,
alongside the nasal release and the flap contact.

DURATIONS.  Rounding transitions up lengthens them, and transitions are spent
out of the same mora budget as everything else, so this had to be checked rather
than assumed.  Over 3,000 dictionary words 91.5% come out the same length to the
frame; the mean moves 1.06 ms on a 615 ms word, 0.17%; the corpus rate goes 7.35
to 7.34 morae/s.  _compensate absorbed it, which is what it is for.

A note on the sweep itself.  It reported nothing but the steps that broke the
limit, so once they were all fixed the "worst remaining" table went blank and
there was no number left to watch drift.  It now records the maximum whatever
its size, which is how the 200 Hz figure above can be quoted at all.

that is not written down gets refitted by whoever last looked at it.

Yazawa & Kondo state theirs, in their 2.3:

    "The start and end boundaries of each vowel token were first determined
    automatically using SPPAS, and then manually modified to correspond to the
    first and last positive zero crossings in Praat.  Vowel duration was
    measured as the time between these boundaries."

That is now the definition, in measure.vowel_duration.  Their stimuli are
reproduced as well -- disyllabic /C1V1C2V2/, five consonantal contexts /bV1p/
/dV1t/ /gV1k/ /zV1s/ /hV1d/, V2 either /e/ or /o/ "to minimize its influence on
V1", accent on the first syllable.  Every one of those contexts keeps the
target vowel voiced, which looks deliberate on their part and means the harness
needs no devoicing special case.

THE TARGET WAS THE WRONG COLUMN.  2.24 is the mean of all ten per-vowel ratios
across MALE AND FEMALE speakers.  Their appendix separates them, and this voice
is male:

              /i/  /e/  /a/  /o/  /u/      mean ratio
    short      68   76   78   74   68
    long      147  156  166  153  150
    ratio    2.16 2.05 2.13 2.07 2.21         2.12   male
                                              2.36   female
                                              2.24   both, which is what this
                                                     file had been fitting to

Where it stands, meaned over the three stop contexts:

              /i/   /e/   /a/   /o/   /u/     mean
    short    71.8  73.1  70.0  68.0  69.0    70.4   -3% on their 73
    long    131.5 132.7 127.2 130.1 131.3   130.6  -15% on their 154
    ratio    1.83  1.82  1.82  1.91  1.90    1.85   against 2.12

The short vowels are right.  The LONG vowels are 15% short, consistently,
across every vowel -- which is not what the frame counting said, and is the
reason for having the measurement rather than a count.

Two of the five contexts have an onset boundary this cannot place, and both are
limits of the measurement and not evidence that the vowel is longer there.
/zV1s/: a voiced fricative is genuinely part-periodic, so there is no instant
at which voicing begins, it is already there.  /hV1d/: our [h] is a VOICELESS
COPY OF THE FOLLOWING VOWEL -- that is how onset() builds it, and it is why /h/
has no locus of its own -- so the two share formants, amplitude envelope, zero
crossing rate and crest factor.  All four were checked; only periodicity
separates them, and that needs a 32 ms window, so the boundary there carries
about +-16 ms.  The three stop contexts agree within 3 ms.

The measurement is built as energy AND low zero-crossing rate AND periodicity,
in that order, each added because the one before it was not enough: energy
alone counted /z/'s frication as vowel and read 138.9 ms against their 78; the
zero-crossing gate fixed /z/ and not /h/; periodicity used as a mask over-
trimmed everything by about 20 ms, because a 32 ms window cannot score high
until it sits wholly inside the vowel.  It therefore SEEDS rather than masks --
the periodic core says where a vowel certainly is, the fine 1 ms grid grows the
boundary outward from it, and the growth is capped at the window's half width,
which is the resolution the periodicity measure actually has.

NOT REFITTED.  LONG_FRAMES is one number and would close most of the 15%, and
that is probably the wrong move.  Yazawa & Kondo's data show vowel identity
matters independently of phonemic length -- their /a/ is the longest vowel and
/i/ and /u/ the shortest, short and long alike -- and they argue against reading
a long vowel as simply twice a short one.  Ours have no vowel-dependent
duration at all: 68-73 ms short and 127-133 long, nearly flat, in close to the
opposite order from theirs.  One timing model with per-vowel targets would
answer both the 15% and the ordering, where LONG_FRAMES += 3 answers neither.
See jp_res/open_questions.md 1.1 and 1.3.

Three things stay separate, and a regression on the ratio alone would collapse
them into one:

    PHONOLOGY          short = 1 mora, long = 2 morae
    TIMING MODEL       mora duration, speaking rate, prosodic position,
                       vowel-dependent realization
    ACOUSTIC MEASURE   first positive zero crossing -> last positive zero
                       crossing

which is why check_vowel_length.py prints per-vowel durations and the ratio
only as a summary line.


Where the missing 24 ms goes
------------------------------

The section above established that long vowels are short and did not say why.
"LONG_FRAMES is wrong" is the tempting conclusion and the wrong shape of one,
because changing it could make the measurement right while leaving the timing
architecture wrong.  The question worth answering first is where the time goes.

THE TARGETS GOT SHARPER FIRST.  Yazawa & Kondo released their per-token data
(JPLongShortVowels.csv, 3,200 tokens), so the harness no longer uses the
paper's rounded appendix.  Two qualifications that the appendix hides:

                   isolated male    embedded male    appendix, pooled
    /i/  /ii/      72.3  152.7      63.2  141.5       68  147
    /e/  /ee/      80.2  164.4      72.2  148.4       76  156
    /a/  /aa/      81.7  173.7      74.4  158.0       78  166
    /o/  /oo/      76.9  159.2      70.6  147.5       74  153
    /u/  /uu/      71.8  155.9      63.7  143.4       68  150

Male, because this voice is male and the two genders differ by about 10%.
ISOLATED, because that is what this harness synthesises -- a word on its own --
and their embedded tokens run 8-10 ms shorter, which is ordinary phrase-medial
shortening.  The appendix pools the two positions, so fitting to it aims
between two conditions and hits neither.  Against the right column the deficit
is larger than it looked: short -8%, long -19%.

THE INVESTIGATION.  Five things could produce a short long vowel.  Measured on
/bape/ against /ba:pe/:

    the second mora is genuinely under-allocated        CONFIRMED
    the renderer terminates early                       no: taper is 3 frames
                                                        in both
    transition smoothing eats part of it                no: the vowel onset is
                                                        frame 5 in both
    the zero-crossing boundary falls early              no: acoustic 129.2 ms
                                                        against 129.2 ms of
                                                        frames, agreeing to
                                                        0.0 ms
    compensation is spending it                         no: both words come out
                                                        exactly on nominal
                                                        budget, 28 and 34
                                                        frames

Everything that differs between the two words is steady vowel at full level:
five frames of it in the short word, ten in the long.  Nothing else moves.

So the increment IS the whole contrast, and it is worth exactly LONG_FRAMES:

      vowel     ours +   theirs +   shortfall
      /i/        59.6      80.4       20.8 ms
      /e/        59.6      84.2       24.6 ms
      /a/        57.2      92.0       34.8 ms
      /o/        62.0      82.3       20.3 ms
      /u/        62.3      84.1       21.8 ms
      mean       60.1      84.6       24.5 ms

LONG_FRAMES = 6, the second mora is budgeted 60 ms, and the increment measures
60.1.  Their increment needs 8.5 frames.

THE ARCHITECTURE IS NOT THE PROBLEM; THE QUANTITY IS.  A long vowel here is
rendered as one continuous vowel held longer -- "extend the target" rather than
two separately articulated morae -- and that is correct.  It is also how they
measure it: one interval, first positive zero crossing to last, with no
internal boundary.  Japanese phonology counts two morae; the acoustics are one
vowel.  Those are different levels and the renderer has the right one.

NO SINGLE VALUE FITS.  Their increment is vowel-dependent: 80.4 ms for /i/ and
92.0 for /a/, which is 8.0 frames against 9.2.  So LONG_FRAMES cannot be set to
one number that satisfies all five, and setting it to 8 or 9 would be the fifth
refit of a constant that should not be a constant.

AND THE TWO ERRORS ARE MOSTLY ONE ERROR.  Split the shortfalls: /a/ is the
outlier at 34.8 ms while the other four sit at 20.3-24.6.  The same split shows
in the short vowels, where /i/ is -1% and /u/ -4% but /a/ is -14% and /e/ -9%.
Our vowels are all about 70 ms because duration here knows nothing about which
vowel it is; theirs run 71.8 to 81.7.  So the picture is:

    a uniform ~2 frames   the second mora is under-allocated
    the rest              vowel-dependent duration, which does not exist here
                          at all, and which is the subject of open_questions 1.3

Raising LONG_FRAMES would close the first and nothing of the second, and would
do it by burying the second inside a constant where the next person cannot see
it.  Hence: diagnosed, not refitted.  Classified as a confirmed acoustic
regression with the root cause assigned -- the second mora's allocation plus
the absent vowel-dependent timing -- and the fix deferred to a timing model
that has per-vowel targets, where both live in the same place.

One thing the measurement does NOT support, and it is worth saying because the
rounded appendix suggested otherwise: the short vowels are not fine.  Against
the pooled appendix they were 3% low and looked clean.  Against the isolated
male column they are 8% low, and /a/ is 14% low.  The long vowels are the
bigger error, not the only one.

Per-vowel duration targets, replacing a fitted constant
---------------------------------------------------------

  check_vowel_length.py       the regression
  95-vowel-duration.wav       the five short vowels, flat then intrinsic
  96-long-vowels.wav          the five long vowels
  97-length-minimal-pairs.wav obasan/obaasan, ojisan/ojiisan, kado/kaado, ...
  98-sentences-vdur.wav       connected speech

Vowel length was a RESIDUAL.  The mora had a fixed budget, the consonant took
what it needed, and the vowel got whatever was left:

    need  = hold + vot + grown + ntr + VOWEL_FLOOR
    total = max(MORA_FRAMES, need)
    nv    = total - hold - vot - grown - ntr

Nothing in that chain knows which vowel it is, which is exactly why every vowel
came out the same length.  And the second mora of a long vowel was one
constant, LONG_FRAMES, refitted four times -- 9 -> 7 -> 6, and it would have
been 8 or 9 again -- against a long/short RATIO measured four different ways.

Now the timing model states DURATIONS, in milliseconds, per vowel, and derives
frames from them.  A ratio is an outcome, not an input.

    V_DUR = {'i': (63.2, 141.5), 'e': (72.2, 148.4), 'a': (74.4, 158.0),
             'o': (70.6, 147.5), 'u': (63.7, 143.4)}

from Yazawa & Kondo's released per-token data, male speakers, EMBEDDED
position.  Embedded rather than isolated because a reader produces connected
text rather than citation forms; their isolated tokens run 8-10 ms longer and
this synthesiser has no position model to switch between the two.  Our short
vowels already matched the embedded mean (70.4 against 68.8) -- the level was
right all along and only the SPREAD was missing.

TWO THINGS HAD TO BE MEASURED RATHER THAN ASSUMED.

First, the target is the WHOLE VOICED VOWEL, not the steady part.  Setting the
steady part alone was tried and inverted the result.  The transition into the
vowel is voiced and at full amplitude, so the measurement counts it, and its
length is _glide_len of the formant travel out of the consonant's locus: after
/b/ that is 5 frames for /i/ and 3 for /u/, because /i/'s F2 has 843 Hz to
cover and /u/'s has 135.  Give both the same steady count and /i/ comes out 9
frames of vowel against /u/'s 7 -- backwards, since those are the two SHORT
vowels in the data.  So the steady part is what remains once the glide is paid
for: nv = want - ntr.  The `qv` line has always said this; it just was not true
of the target.

Second, the frames-to-milliseconds conversion.  A frame is 10 ms and the
measurement loses a fixed tail at the end, where the taper into the following
consonant decays below the threshold.  Forced the allocation from 7 to 12
frames and read back what the measurement reports, over five vowels and three
stop contexts -- 30 cells:

    allocated      7     8     9    10    11    12   frames
    measured    50.3  60.5  67.7  81.9  89.5  99.5   ms, mean of five vowels
    implied     19.7  19.5  22.3  18.1  20.5  20.5   ms of tail

20 ms, flat with length, which is what a fixed taper should give.  An earlier
17 was taken from two points, measured badly, and put /oo/ a frame short.  /a/
is the one exception at 25 ms -- its formants sit furthest from most consonant
loci, so more of its tail is spent gliding at falling amplitude before the
threshold catches it.  Measured, stable across allocations, and given its own
value rather than smeared into the mean where it would cost every other vowel a
millisecond to flatter one.

RESULT, by their definition over their stimuli:

                  ours    theirs           ours    theirs
      /i/ short   59.5     63.2    long   132.6    141.5
      /e/         69.5     72.2           142.4    148.4
      /a/         77.1     74.4           150.9    158.0
      /o/         68.0     70.6           141.0    147.5
      /u/         61.0     63.7           134.2    143.4
      mean        67.0     68.8    -3%    140.2    147.7   -5%

      ordering    /a/ > /e/ > /o/ > /u/ > /i/   short AND long
      theirs      /a/ > /e/ > /o/ > /u/ > /i/   exactly

Every vowel within 6%, where before they were flat at 68-73 and 127-133 in
close to the opposite order.  The corpus rate is 7.30 morae/s against 7.34
before, so this did not buy length by slowing the voice down.

WHAT IS STILL APPROXIMATE.  A 10 ms frame against an 11 ms spread: the
quantiser can express two levels across the five vowels, not five.  The data
falls into two groups -- the high vowels /i/ and /u/ at 63 ms, the other three
at 70-74 -- so the distinction that survives is the one that is actually there,
and the residual is the rounding.  A shorter frame would carry more of it.

AND WHAT THIS DELIBERATELY DOES NOT DO.  It does not put "long = 2.1 x short"
anywhere.  The ratio comes out at 2.09 against their 2.15 because the durations
are right, not because a multiplier was applied.  The three levels stay
separate:

    PHONOLOGY          short = 1 mora, long = 2 morae
    TIMING MODEL       V_DUR, plus rate, prosodic position and the mora budget
    ACOUSTIC MEASURE   first positive zero crossing -> last positive zero
                       crossing

The remaining uniform few percent is a RATE question, which is the one knob in
the middle layer this synthesiser still does not have -- see
jp_res/open_questions.md.


Word-final /N/, which was not a nasal at all
----------------------------------------------

   99-final-n.wav        obasan ojisan obaasan ojiisan, paired
  100-final-n-words.wav  nihon mikan kaban shinbun sensei hon san gohan
  101-n-three-ways.wav   before a vowel / homorganic / final

Reported by ear on 97: "is it trying to say Obasan and Ojisan?  The N sounds
like a softer A."  It was.  moraic_n() returned None for three different
situations and the /N/ branch gave all three the same treatment -- keep the
preceding vowel's posture, widen the bandwidths, drop 8 dB:

    /obasan/   F1 736  F2 1228   held for the whole final mora

which is the /a/ in front of it, quieter.  Two of the three situations are
right: before a vowel and before /h/ there is no oral closure and the
realisation IS a nasalised vowel.  Utterance-final is not that case.  The final
allophone is uvular, there is a real constriction, and the formants have
somewhere to go.  Folding it in with the other two left final /N/ with nasal
AMPLITUDE and no nasal RESONANCE, and the murmur is the whole reason a nasal
sounds like one.

The murmur was already there and already sourced -- N1 250-300, N2 ~1000 from
the general literature -- and every /N/ before a consonant has always used it.
Only the final one bypassed it, because `csp is None` was being read as "no
murmur either" when it only means "no place transition".

    /obasan/   F1 664 -> 300 over five frames, F2 held at 1228
               then the murmur, F1 272 F2 1004, at murmur amplitude

F1 does the closing, which is place-independent and real.  F2 stays where the
vowel left it.  Borrowing the velar row for the uvular place was tried first
and points the WRONG WAY: the velar locus for /a/ is 1530, above /a/'s own
1225, so the velar pinch raises F2 going into the nasal where a uvular -- which
is further back than a velar, not forward of it -- has to lower it.  That is
not an approximation, it is the opposite sign.  There is no uvular locus
measurement to hand, and final /N/ has no place contrast to signal anyway,
being the only nasal that occurs there, so nothing is lost by declining to
guess.


The moraic nasal before a vowel, lost in translation
------------------------------------------------------

Found while building 101, which needed a word with /N/ before a vowel.  There
was not one: kana_to_romaji() flattens kana to a romaji STRING and the parser
then re-tokenises it, so an unmarked `n` before a vowel is swallowed by the
vowel after it.

    renai  (love, れんあい)   ->  ['re', 'na', 'i']

The word lost its nasal AND a mora.  Hepburn's apostrophe exists for exactly
this and the parser already understood it -- "ren'ai" always parsed correctly;
nothing ever emitted it.  Now it does, before a vowel or a y-mora:

    gen'in   -> ['ge', 'N', 'i', 'N']      cause
    tan'i    -> ['ta', 'N', 'i']           unit
    shin'you -> ['shi', 'N', 'yo', ':']    trust

Over 40,000 dictionary pronunciations this affects 215 words, 0.54%.  Small,
and every one of them was wrong in two ways at once.


11025 Hz, which is what TruVoice actually shipped
---------------------------------------------------

  render_11k.py        renders every sample at 11025 into "11 kHz/"
  11 kHz/              73 files, the same samples at the native rate

All of this was developed at 16 kHz.  include/tvtts.h is explicit that 11025 is
"the engine's native rate", "what TruVoice shipped as its desktop rate", so the
11 kHz rendering is what anyone running TruVoice will hear.  Rendered natively
rather than resampled: Nyquist falls from 8000 to 5512, which is inside the
band the sibilants and bursts live in, and resampling afterwards would
low-pass that away instead of letting the engine do whatever it does.

Frame timing is rate-independent -- a frame is 10 ms either way -- so durations,
the sweep and the vowel-length regression stand at both rates.

AND IT TURNED UP A LEVEL PROBLEM, which took three tries to characterise.

At 11025 one sample clips outright and the next is 0.1 dB from it.  The first
reading of that was "our frames drive the engine about 6 dB harder than its own
English front end does" -- comparing our loudest sample against the engine's
English on a different utterance, which is not a comparison at all.  It is
wrong and is corrected here.

The engine's output is not hot in general.  /konnichiwa/ peaks at 10208 at
11025 against the engine's own English front end at 11328 on the same rate, and
our median sample peak at 16 kHz is 11208 against its 10064.  The level is
right where it should be.

What is hot is one specific class of segment, and only at the native rate:

    peak in the noise frames, aspiration excluded, on words that do not devoice

                    16 kHz    11 kHz    change
       /ts/           7744     21848     +9.0 dB
       /s/            8024     19720     +7.8
       /t/            7432     16616     +7.0
       [C] sh         7112     14536     +6.2
       /ch/           7480     14656     +5.8
       /k/            7600      6709     -1.1
       /p/            7552      7472     -0.1
       vowels                             +0.9

The affected set is exactly the segments whose noise posture parks F3 and F4 at
the engine's 4000/4080 Hz ceiling and carries the noise on band 6 -- SIB_POST
and BURST_SRC.  /k/ and /p/ use their own burst postures without the ceiling
and are untouched, and so are the vowels.  At 16 kHz, 4080 Hz sits at 0.51 of
Nyquist; at 11025 it is at 0.74, where a digital resonator's gain is a good
deal higher.  The trick that places a sibilant peak the cascade cannot
otherwise reach stops being free when the band shrinks under it.

A uniform trim on bands 5, 6 and track 8 was tried and is not the fix: -24
brings /s/, /t/, [C] and /ch/ within a decibel but leaves /ts/ at +6.8, which
says the problem is the posture rather than the gain.  The real fix is a
rate-aware SIB_POST -- F3/F4 scaled to the band rather than pinned at 4080 --
and that is a deliberate change, not an end-of-session one.  Recorded in
jp_res/open_questions.md.

TWO MEASUREMENT TRAPS, both hit on the way here and both worth remembering.  A
frame is 160 samples at 16 kHz and 110 at 11025, so slicing by a hardcoded 160
reads the wrong part of the signal at the native rate.  And /shishi/ devoices
both its vowels, so its "noise frames" are mostly the devoiced vowel's
aspiration and not the fricative at all -- the sibilant measurements above use
/shasha/ and exclude any frame carrying track 2.


/v/, which was a hole in two places at once
---------------------------------------------

The romaji reader accepted `v` as an onset and nothing ever gave it a MANNER
row or a LOCUS row, so `va` fell through to manner 'none' and rendered as a
bare vowel with no consonant at all.  And katakana VU was absent from the kana
table altogether, so from kana the character was silently DROPPED:

    violin, in katakana   ->   'iorin'

Japanese has no /v/.  The kana was invented in the 20th century to transcribe
a foreign one and most speakers say /b/, so that is what both paths now do:

    violin   -> baiorin   ['ba', 'i', 'o', 'ri', 'N']
    Verdi    -> berudi    ['be', 'ru', 'di']

MANNER['v'] and LOCUS['v'] alias /b/ so the romaji path agrees with the kana
one rather than leaving the hole in only one of them.


The reference did not match the measurement
---------------------------------------------

Astra reviewed the vowel-length work and found a methodological error that
reverses its conclusion.  Every claim below was checked against the dataset and
the code before being accepted; all of them hold.

THE COMPARISON WAS MISMATCHED.  check_vowel_length.py averages OUR output over
/bVp/, /dVt/ and /gVk/ -- the three of Yazawa & Kondo's five contexts whose
boundaries it can place -- and compared that against targets averaged over all
FIVE.  A three-context mean against a five-context mean is not a comparison.
Restricted to the same three:

                  all five            the three measured
    /i/  /ii/     63.2  141.5         67.8  144.7
    /e/  /ee/     72.2  148.4         74.0  148.0
    /a/  /aa/     74.4  158.0         76.3  155.0
    /o/  /oo/     70.6  147.5         69.1  144.0
    /u/  /uu/     63.7  143.4         65.9  141.6

and the residual stops being uniform:

              /i/    /e/    /a/    /o/    /u/
    short    -12%    -6%    +1%    -2%    -7%
    long      -8%    -4%    -3%    -2%    -5%

/a/ runs the opposite way from the rest.  The "uniform -3%/-5%, therefore rate"
conclusion was an artefact of the mismatched reference, and would not have
identified rate even had it held: a uniform error is equally consistent with a
shared measurement or rendering bias.

V_DUR keeps the five-context means and TARGET now takes the three-context ones.
Those are different numbers for different jobs -- the first is the timing
model's estimate of a vowel's intrinsic duration, where averaging over more
contexts is better; the second is a measurement reference and has to match what
the harness can actually measure.  Conflating them is what produced the error.

The stale labels went with it: the TARGET block still said "ISOLATED position"
in its comment while holding embedded values.

AND THE RESIDUAL TRACKS THE MEASUREMENT, NOT THE MODEL.  Auditing the boundary
detector, as Astra asked, turned up that its documented knob does nothing:

    onset_db     14     17     20     23     26
    /i/        59.5   59.5   59.5   59.5   59.5     ms, unchanged
    /a/        77.1   77.1   77.1   77.1   77.1

So does the periodicity threshold, over 0.35 to 0.55.  What actually places the
boundary is the GROWTH CAP -- the limit on how far the fine grid may grow out
from the periodic core, an engineering constant with no phonetic content -- and
the vowels respond to it unequally:

    cap         8 ms    16 ms    32 ms      range
    /i/         50.9     59.5     64.6     13.7 ms
    /a/         74.9     77.1     77.1      2.2 ms

Correlating that range (ms, over an 8-32 ms cap) against the short-vowel error
(-12.3, -6.1, +1.0, -1.5, -7.4 %) gives r = -0.982, n = 5.

What that supports is a measurement-confound diagnosis, not a complete
attribution, and the entry has been reworded to say so.  Five vowel identities
is a very small sample; both quantities come out of the SAME detector, so they
are not independent; and sensitivity to an arbitrary 8-32 ms range is not a
calibrated uncertainty interval.  The +0.15 against transition length does not
exclude a phonetic contribution either.  The residual is strongly associated
with sensitivity to the growth cap, which makes boundary placement a major
confound: the current measurements cannot separate synthesis error from
detector bias.  Rate is not excluded; attributing the residual to it was.

That settles the order of work, which is Astra's: fix the comparison, audit the
boundaries, and only then consider rate -- as a relative control with 1.0
preserving the present baseline, applied before frame allocation and covering
consonants and transitions, since changing MORA_FRAMES alone will no longer do
it now that vowel durations are assigned independently.  Position comes after
that, as a separately calibrated factor rather than folded into V_DUR: isolated
minus embedded is +6.3 to +9.0 ms for short vowels and +11.1 to +16.0 for long,
so it is not one constant, and the measured vowel is the word's FIRST, which
makes it an isolation effect rather than phrase-final lengthening.

One more thing the audit exposed: all five long-vowel increments round to the
same 8 frames.  That does NOT erase the vowel-specific long durations -- those
are still 16/17/18/17/16 frames, because the short baselines differ -- so what
quantisation costs is the vowel-specific LENGTHENING AMOUNT, not the long
duration itself.  (An earlier draft of this paragraph said the distinction was
not expressed at all, which was wrong.)  Reported mean errors are about 2 ms
short and 7.5 ms long, both inside one 10 ms quantum; that does not make them
negligible, since averaging many tokens can expose systematic bias below the
frame interval.  It does mean quantisation has to be dealt with before a few
milliseconds can be read as anything.


2026-10-04: assisted detector and stimulus verification
----------------------------------------------------
The diagnostic is separate from measure.py and has explicit failure statuses.
It now actually uses its periodicity features to qualify an energetic core.
Search limits do not clip components into apparently valid durations. A
component reaching another supplied vowel seed is rejected as merged; failure
at any cutoff makes the across-cutoff result indeterminate. Valid sensitivity
ranges are descriptive, not uncertainty intervals or agreement with the paper.

Measurement uses untrimmed, unfaded PCM. Synth_ResetTracks in src/engine/synth.c
initializes 12 frames before caller input; raw_frame_sample() maps schedule
coordinates through that prefix. This is control timing, not acoustic truth.

2026-10-05: a geminated fricative is not a closure
----------------------------------------------------
Reported by ear: スラッシュ came out as "surachu" -- the /sh/ too short, and the
word landing on an affricate where every other system has a long fricative.

Measured, the geminate was 14 frames in which nothing sounded at all, followed
by 10 frames of /ʃ/ -- the same 10 the SINGLETON /s/ of its first mora gets.
So the geminate contributed 140 ms of silence and no frication whatever, and
silence followed by a short fricative is the acoustic signature of an
AFFRICATE.  The ear was reporting the thing exactly.

The geminate branch had one shape for every consonant: taper the vowel, then
MORA_FRAMES of track 0 at zero.  For /kk/ /tt/ /pp/ that is right and it is the
length cue Yanagisawa & Arai measured -- their crossover, 184 ms of closure
with the preceding transition against 240 without, is a claim about a silence.
A fricative has no closure phase to be silent IN.  /ʃː/ is one long noise.

Yamakawa & Amano settle the shape, and the trapezoid in _fric_env is already
theirs: a rise of about 40%, a steady part, a decay, "so the shape scales and
ONLY THE TOTAL LENGTH distinguishes a fricative from an affricate."  A geminate
fricative is therefore the same trapezoid over roughly twice the frames.

So the trapezoid is now SPLIT ACROSS THE TWO MORAE: the geminate raises the
noise and holds it, the mora after it holds and lets it go.  _fric_env grew a
fall_frac to go with its rise_frac, and either end can be dropped.  One shared
_fric_noise picks the source and track for both halves, because a disagreement
between them would be a step in the middle of one continuous sound.

   スラッシュ, the geminate and the mora after it

                        before              after
     frames 29-42     silence            noise rising 21 -> 36
     frames 43-52     noise 23 -> 36     noise 36, then 32, 28
     frication          100 ms             240 ms
     total frames        60                 60

NOTHING ABOUT THE TIMING MOVED.  Frame counts and mora end times are identical
-- the tests assert it -- so the mora budget, the pitch contour and the vowel
durations are all untouched.  Only what fills the slot changed.

Over the 3,312 naist-jdic readings that have a geminate before a fricative --
0.68% of the dictionary, /s/ 1774 and /sh/ 1501 of them -- none now contains a
silent frame inside the geminate, against all 3,312 before.  The frication
against the same consonant ungeminated goes from a median ratio of 1.00, which
is to say the geminate was adding nothing, to 2.40.  For comparison the stop
closures here run 50 ms singleton against 190 geminate.

A geminated STOP is untouched and still a silence; 学校 and 切手 render sample
for sample as they did.  Three oracle words moved: zasshi, いっせんかい and
